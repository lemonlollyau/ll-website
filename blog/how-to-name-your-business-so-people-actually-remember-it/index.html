How to name your business so people actually remember it

By the lemonlolly team·1 August 2026·5 min read

You're at a café on The Parade, notebook open, listing name ideas for the business you're about to launch. Twenty minutes in you've either got a shortlist you love or a page of words that all sound like accounting firms. Naming feels like it should be the fun part, and then somehow it becomes the thing that stalls the whole launch.

Here's the reassuring bit: a memorable name isn't luck, and it isn't reserved for people with a marketing degree. It's a handful of rules, a few traps to sidestep, and the discipline to stop fiddling once you've got a good one. This is how to name a business so it actually sticks in people's heads — and how to check it's yours to use before you order the signage.

Why the name earns its keep

Your business name is the single word or phrase that has to survive being said out loud in a noisy pub, spelled down a dodgy phone line, and typed into Google by someone who heard it once from a mate. It's on your van, your invoices, your Instagram handle and the tip of a happy customer's tongue when they recommend you. A name that's clear and easy to repeat does quiet marketing for you every single day. A clumsy one makes every referral a little bit harder.

That doesn't mean the name is everything. Plenty of forgettable-sounding businesses do brilliantly because the work is great and the service is warm. But when you're starting from zero, a name that's easy to say and easy to remember removes friction at exactly the moment you can least afford it.

Five rules for a name that sticks

None of these are laws. They're the patterns behind names that keep working years down the track.

  • Say it before you settle it. If you stumble over your own name out loud, so will everyone else. Read the shortlist to someone who's never heard it and watch their face.
  • Keep it short and spellable. One or two easy words beat a clever compound nobody can type. If you have to spell it every time you say it, it's costing you.
  • Leave yourself room to grow. "Adelaide Deck Repairs" boxes you in the day you start building pergolas. A slightly broader name lets the business change without a rebrand.
  • Make it feel like something. The best names carry a bit of personality — warmth, cheek, calm, craft. A name that hints at how you do business is doing double duty.
  • Check it works small. It has to read on a phone screen, a coffee cup and a social avatar. If it only looks good huge on a billboard, it's the wrong shape.

The three traps that kill good names

Most naming regrets trace back to one of these. They all feel clever in the moment.

  • The inside joke. A name that only makes sense once you've explained the story is a name you'll be explaining forever. Save the backstory for your About page, not the front door.
  • The trend chase. Dropped vowels, a random "-ly", a forced misspelling — they date fast and they wreck word-of-mouth, because nobody can guess how it's written. In five years it'll read like a time stamp.
  • The too-narrow box. Naming yourself after one suburb or one service feels focused, but it quietly caps the business. Pick a name that can still be true after you expand.

Pressure-test your shortlist

Once you've got three or four you like, run each one through the same quick checks before you fall in love. A simple grid keeps you honest — score each name and the winner usually picks itself.

Check it's actually yours to use

You've found the one. Before you commit, five minutes of homework saves a world of pain later. Search the ASIC business names register to see if it's registered, then check the trade marks register on IP Australia — a name can be free to register as a business name but still bump into someone else's trade mark. Do a plain Google search, then check the socials and whether a sensible domain is available.

If the exact .com is gone, don't panic and don't ditch a great name over it. A .com.au, a slightly longer domain, or adding your city works fine. The name should lead; the domain follows. And when you're ready to lock in the web presence, it's worth building a site that actually earns enquiries — we broke that down in what makes a small business website actually convert.

One honest caveat: this is a sensible first pass, not legal advice. If a lot is riding on the name, a short conversation with a trade marks lawyer is cheap insurance.

Know when to stop

Naming has a way of expanding to fill all the time you give it. At some point the deliberating stops adding value and starts costing you momentum.

A good-enough name you launch beats a perfect name you're still workshopping in six months. The name grows meaning through the work you do under it — not the other way around.

Think about it: "lemonlolly" doesn't literally describe marketing. It earned its meaning by turning up and doing good work with a bit of zing. Your name will do the same once real customers attach real experiences to it. Pick the strongest option on your grid, sit with it for a day, and then commit.

Once you've landed on it

A name is the anchor, not the whole brand. Next comes the look and voice that hang off it — and that's where a clear brief pays off.
