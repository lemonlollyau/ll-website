Your tagline is doing nothing — here's how to fix it

By the lemonlolly team·11 August 2026·5 min read

Picture a tradie's van parked on Prospect Road. Business name, phone number, and underneath it in italics: "Quality you can trust." Two doors down, another van: "Your partner in success." A café on Unley Road with "Where quality meets passion" on the window.

None of those lines is doing a scrap of work. You could peel them off and stick them on any of the other ten thousand businesses in Adelaide and they'd fit just as snugly. That's the problem with most taglines — they're not wrong, exactly. They're just wallpaper. Pleasant, forgettable, and utterly interchangeable.

The good news is that a tagline is one of the cheapest things to fix in your whole brand. You don't need a designer or a budget. You need about an afternoon and a willingness to say something real.

What a tagline is actually for

A tagline has one job: to tell a stranger, in a handful of words, why you're worth a second glance. It's the line that sits next to your logo and does the talking your logo can't. A logo is a mark; a tagline is a sentence of meaning attached to it.

It's not a mission statement. It's not a summary of everything you do. It's the one idea you'd want to lodge in someone's head if you only got one go — because on a van, a sign, or a search result, one go is usually all you get.

Done well, it does something no logo can: it makes a promise, or draws a line, or plants a feeling. "We do X for Y." "The Z way of doing it." Something a person could repeat to a mate over coffee. If nobody could repeat yours, it isn't earning the space it's taking up.

Why most taglines say nothing

Almost every dud tagline fails in the same way: it reaches for words that sound like business and mean nothing. Quality. Passion. Trust. Excellence. Solutions. Partner. These feel like the right register — professional, safe, grown-up — which is exactly why everyone lands on them and exactly why they've stopped meaning anything.

Here's the test. Read your tagline, then imagine your closest competitor putting it on their sign. Does it still fit? If it does, it's not describing you — it's describing your industry. And "generic description of my industry" is not a reason for anyone to choose you.

If your competitor could use your tagline without changing a word, you don't have a tagline. You have a gap where one should be.

The other common failure is trying to cram everything in. A plumber who does "hot water, gas fitting, blocked drains, renovations and emergency callouts" tries to list all five and ends up with a mouthful nobody remembers. A tagline isn't a menu. It's a hook. You pick the one thing worth being known for and let the rest live on your services page.

Tagline, slogan, strapline — sorting the words out

People use these three terms interchangeably, which muddies the water. They're related but not the same, and knowing the difference stops you writing the wrong one.

TermWhat it doesHow long it lasts
TaglineSums up what your brand is about, sits near the logoYears — it's part of your identity
SloganSells one campaign, offer or promotionWeeks or months, then it's retired
StraplineBritish word for tagline — same thing, different accentSame as a tagline

For a small business, the tagline is the one that matters. Slogans are for when you've got a specific promotion to push. Most Adelaide businesses we meet don't need a slogan at all — they need one good tagline that's been quietly pulling its weight for years.

A simple formula for writing one

You don't need to be clever. Clever is a bonus, not the goal — clear beats clever every time. Work through these in order and you'll land somewhere honest, which is already ahead of most.

  • Name who you help, or what you do differently. "Small-batch bread for people who notice the difference." "The bookkeeper who actually rings you back." Specific is memorable; vague is invisible.
  • Say the result, not the activity. People don't want "professional accounting services." They want to stop worrying about the ATO. Point at the outcome they're after.
  • Use words you'd say out loud. If you wouldn't tell a customer "we deliver holistic solutions" across the counter, don't put it under your logo. Read it aloud — if it feels stiff, it is.
  • Cut until it stings a little. Write the long version first, then hack it down to three to seven words. The tighter it gets, the more each word has to earn its place.

Write ten. Not one — ten. The first three will be the obvious clichés, and that's fine; getting them out of your system clears the way. Somewhere around number seven you'll start saying something that actually sounds like you. That's the one to keep circling.

The one test before you commit

Once you've got a favourite, run it past a real person — ideally a customer, not your business partner. Say your business name, then the tagline, and watch their face. If they nod like they've understood something, you're there. If they say "nice" in the polite way people say "nice" about a baby that isn't cute, keep going.

A tagline is only worth writing if it earns its place, and it only earns its place by being clearer or truer or more specific than the silence it replaces. A blank space says nothing. A bad tagline says nothing while taking up room. A good one says the single thing you most want remembered — which is a lot to ask of five words, and exactly why it's worth the afternoon.

Where it fits in the bigger picture

A tagline doesn't stand on its own. It's the shortest expression of your brand voice and your positioning — so if those are fuzzy, the tagline will be too. If you're not sure what your brand actually stands for yet, start with what a brand actually is and work forward from there. The tagline is the last five per cent, not the first.

But it's a visible five per cent. It's on your van, your footer, your Google listing, the first line of your ads. Fixing it is a small job with an outsized payoff — the rare bit of branding you can do this week, for free, and feel the difference.

Tagline questions, answered

A good tagline says something only your business could say. It either names who you help, the result you deliver, or the thing you do differently — and it does it in words a real person would use out loud. If you could swap your competitor's name in and it still fits, it isn't a tagline, it's wallpaper.

Short enough to remember after hearing it once — usually three to seven words. There's no hard rule, but the longer it gets, the more it drifts toward a sentence nobody keeps. If you can't say it in one breath without losing the thread, trim it until you can.

A slogan is usually tied to one campaign or promotion and changes as the campaign does. Most small businesses need a solid tagline first and can worry about slogans later, if ever.
