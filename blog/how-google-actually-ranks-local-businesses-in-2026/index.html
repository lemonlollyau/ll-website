How Google actually ranks local businesses in 2026

By the lemonlolly team·31 July 2026·9 min read

Picture two plumbers on the same street in Prospect. Same trade, same prices, both good at the job. Someone three suburbs over types "emergency plumber near me" into their phone at 9pm. One of them shows up in the little map at the top of the results with a row of gold stars. The other is on page three, where nobody looks. Same street, wildly different night.

The difference isn't luck, and it usually isn't the fancier website either. It's that one of them has quietly stacked up the exact things Google uses to decide who to show. The reassuring part is that those things aren't a secret — Google has told us what they are. The frustrating part is that almost nobody reads past the headline. So let's actually read it.

The three things Google is weighing

When Google decides which local businesses to show for a search like "cafe near me" or "electrician Norwood", it says it's balancing three factors: relevance, distance and prominence. That's it. Everything you'll ever read about local SEO — the profile tips, the review campaigns, the suburb pages — is really just a way of nudging one of those three.

Think of it like a pub quiz where Google is the host deciding who gets called up. Relevance is whether you actually know the answer to the question asked. Distance is how close you're sitting to the front. Prominence is whether the room already knows and trusts you. You want to be strong on all three, but they don't count equally for every search — and knowing which one to work on is most of the game.

You can't argue your way to the top of the map. You can only become, on paper and in reality, the most relevant, closest, best-known answer to the question being asked.

Relevance: does your listing match what they typed

Relevance is how well your business matches the search. It sounds obvious, but it's the factor businesses most often leave money on the table with, because they assume Google can work out what they do. It can't read your mind — it reads your profile and your website, and it takes them fairly literally.

The biggest lever here is your Google Business Profile categories. Your primary category is one of the strongest relevance signals you have, so it needs to be the most specific accurate description of what you do — "Thai restaurant", not just "restaurant"; "emergency plumber" if that's genuinely your bread and butter. Then you add the secondary categories that also fit. Most businesses pick one vague category and stop, and they wonder why they don't show for the specific things they're best at.

After categories, relevance comes from:

  • The services and products you list on your profile, described in the words your customers actually use.
  • Your business description, written for a human but honest about what you offer.
  • The content on your website — the pages, the headings, the plain descriptions of what you do and where.
  • Regular posts and photos that keep the profile looking alive rather than abandoned.

None of this is about tricking anyone. It's about removing every reason for Google to be unsure what you do. If you sell three things and only mention one, you've made yourself irrelevant for two-thirds of your own business. Distance: closer than you think, and you can't fake it

Distance is the simplest of the three and the one you have the least control over. Google works out roughly where the searcher is — from their phone's location or the town they typed — and measures how far you are from it. Closer usually wins, all else being equal. This is why the same search gives different results depending on which end of Adelaide you're standing at.

You can't pick up your shopfront and move it to the CBD, and you shouldn't try to fake an address you don't work from — that's the fastest way to get a listing suspended. But distance isn't quite as fixed as it feels, because it interacts with the other two factors. A business that's a little further away but far more prominent and relevant will often still beat a closer, weaker one. Strength buys you range.

The honest lever here is service areas and genuine location content. If you actually work across the northern suburbs, say so, and back it with real pages about the work you do there rather than a thin doorway page for every postcode. We wrote a whole piece on why Adelaide businesses go missing from Google Maps — distance problems are behind a surprising number of them, and half the fixes are things people don't realise they've got wrong.

Prominence: the one you actually build

Prominence is how well known and well regarded your business is, and it's the factor with the most headroom for most small businesses. Google pieces it together from all over the web: your reviews, links to your site, mentions of your name and address, articles about you, and how complete and active your profile is. In plain terms, it's asking "does the wider internet treat this business like it's real and reputable?"

Reviews are the loudest prominence signal you can influence directly. Google looks at how many you have, how good they are, how recent, and whether you bother to reply. A slow, steady stream of genuine reviews beats a sudden pile that then dries up — and it certainly beats bought ones, which Google has got good at sniffing out. If asking for reviews makes you squirm, we have a gentle system for it in how to get more Google reviews without being annoying.

Beyond reviews, prominence grows from the slow, boring, real stuff: being listed accurately in local directories, getting mentioned by other Adelaide sites and organisations, a website that other people actually link to, and consistency of your name, address and phone number everywhere they appear. It's less a switch you flip and more a reputation you accumulate. That's annoying if you want an overnight result, and it's brilliant if you're willing to do the work your competitors won't.

The three factors at a glance

FactorWhat it meansThe levers you can pull
RelevanceHow well you match the searchRight primary category, full services list, clear website content
DistanceHow close you are to the searcherGenuine service areas, honest suburb pages, correct verified location
ProminenceHow known and trusted you appearSteady reviews, replies, links, mentions, consistent details

Read that table as a to-do list, not a report card. Pick the row where you're weakest and start there — it's almost always the one with the most to gain.

What doesn't move the needle (and can backfire)

Because the three factors are simple, a lot of energy gets wasted on things that either do nothing or actively hurt. A few worth naming plainly:

  • Stuffing keywords into your business name. Calling yourself "Joe's Plumbing Adelaide Emergency 24/7 Best Plumber" is against Google's guidelines and can get you suspended. Use your real business name.
  • Buying reviews or trading them. Short-term shine, long-term risk. Fake reviews get detected and removed, and the account can be penalised.
  • Fake addresses or a rented mailbox in a suburb you don't work. This is the number one cause of hard-to-recover suspensions.
  • Obsessing over your website while ignoring your profile. For local pack results, the profile often does more heavy lifting than the site — though the site still matters for relevance and for turning clicks into customers.

The through-line: anything that tries to fake one of the three factors tends to unwind. Anything that genuinely improves one of them tends to compound.

Where to start this week

If all of this feels like a lot, don't try to do it at once. The order that works best for most Adelaide businesses is: fix relevance first because it's quick and within your control, shore up distance honestly with real service-area content, then commit to the long game of prominence.

Concretely, this week: open your Google Business Profile and check your primary category is the most specific accurate one, fill in every service you offer, and send three past customers a friendly request for a review. That's a morning's work and it touches all three factors. From there, it's maintenance and patience.
