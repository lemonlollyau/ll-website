Rebranding: when it's worth it and when it's a distraction

By the lemonlolly team·9 August 2026·5 min read

It usually starts on a slow afternoon. You're staring at your own logo for the hundredth time this week, it's looking a bit tired, and a thought lands: maybe we should rebrand. New name, new look, fresh start. By Friday you've got a mood board and a quote that's made your eyes water.

Slow down. A rebrand is one of the most expensive things a small business can do to itself, and half the time the thing you're bored of is the thing your customers use to recognise you. Sometimes a rebrand is exactly right and pays for itself for a decade. Sometimes it's a very costly way of avoiding a harder problem. This is the gut-check to tell which one you're looking at — before you spend the money.

First, get your terms straight

Most people say "rebrand" when they mean "refresh," and the two cost wildly different amounts. A refresh tidies what's already there — a cleaner logo, a tightened palette, better photos, sharper words — and your customers still know it's you. A rebrand changes what you're fundamentally known as: a new name, a new position, a new promise.

A refresh is redecorating the shop. A rebrand is changing the sign over the door and hoping everyone still walks in.

That distinction matters because the risk lives almost entirely on the rebrand side. If what's bugging you is that things look a bit dated, you probably want a refresh — cheaper, quicker, and it keeps the recognition you've already built. Before you go further, it helps to be clear on what a brand actually is, because it's a lot more than the logo you're tired of.

When a rebrand is genuinely worth it

There are a handful of situations where changing the whole thing is the right call rather than an indulgence. Each of these is a problem the brand itself is causing:

  • The name actively confuses people. It's hard to spell, hard to say, sounds like a competitor, or describes something you no longer do. "Adelaide Hills Pool Fencing" is a problem the day you start doing decks and pergolas too.
  • You've outgrown your position. You started as the cheap, cheerful option and now you do premium work — but the brand still whispers "budget," and it's capping what you can charge. That's a positioning problem the current brand can't carry.
  • Something structural changed. A merger, a buyout, splitting one business into two, or a genuine change in what you sell. The old brand no longer describes the company that exists.
  • Your reputation is damaged and it's tied to the name. Rare, and a rebrand won't fix bad service — but if the name itself carries baggage, a clean start can be part of the answer.

Notice the pattern: in every case, the brand is holding the business back in a way you can point at. That's the test. If you can finish the sentence "we're losing money or opportunities because our brand…" with something concrete, you're probably in worth-it territory.

When it's a distraction

Now the other list — the reasons that feel urgent but are usually the business avoiding a harder conversation. A rebrand is a satisfying project. It's visible, it's creative, and it feels like progress. Which is exactly why it's such a tempting place to hide.

  • You're bored. You've looked at your brand ten thousand times. Your customers have seen it maybe twice. Boredom is the most expensive reason to rebrand, because you feel the fatigue years before anyone else would.
  • A competitor got a slick new look. Their rebrand is not a strategy for your business. Copying it just makes you the second-best version of them.
  • Sales are quiet and you want to do something. If the phone's not ringing, the problem is almost never the colour of your logo. New paint on a car that won't start gets you nowhere.
  • One loud person said they didn't like it. One opinion is not a mandate. Ask ten customers before you touch anything.

None of these mean your marketing is fine — quiet sales are a real problem. They just mean a rebrand is the wrong tool. The fix is usually further down the funnel: your offer, your website, how you're getting found. A rebrand papers over that, expensively, and buys you a few months of feeling busy.

When the itch strikes, run the signal past this before you do anything:

If you do rebrand, do it in the right order

Say you've genuinely got a worth-it reason. The mistake now is to start with the logo, because the logo is the last decision, not the first. Work in this order: nail the position (who you're for and why you're the right choice), then the voice — how you sound is half your brand, and you can sort it out fast with our guide to finding your brand voice in an afternoon — and only then the look. Design decisions made before the strategy is settled get thrown out twice.

And plan the changeover like a move, not a reveal. Every place your old name lives — website, Google Business Profile, signage, invoices, socials, that directory listing from 2019 — needs updating, ideally in a tight window so you're not half one thing and half another for months.

The cost nobody puts on the invoice

Here's the part that gets skipped in the excitement. When you change your name or web address, you don't just pay the designer — you spend down the recognition and search history you've quietly banked for years. Google treats a new domain as a near-stranger until you've mapped every old page to its new home with redirects, and even then it takes time to earn the trust back.

That's recoverable with a proper migration. But it's a real cost, it belongs in your decision, and it's the reason "I fancied a change" isn't a good enough reason. A rebrand should buy you something bigger than it costs — a name you can grow into, a position you can charge for, a business the old brand couldn't describe. If it can't clear that bar, it's a distraction wearing a nice new logo.

Rebranding questions, answered

A refresh tidies what's already there — a cleaner version of the logo, a tightened colour palette, better photography, sharper copy. Your customers still recognise you. A rebrand changes what you're fundamentally known as: a new name, a new position in the market, a new promise. A refresh is redecorating the shop; a rebrand is changing the sign over the door and hoping people still walk in. Most businesses that think they need a rebrand actually need a refresh.

Ask whether the problem is with your brand or with your business. If your name genuinely confuses people, your position no longer matches what you sell, you're merging or splitting, or you've outgrown a name that boxes you in, a rebrand earns its keep. If you're just bored, chasing a competitor's new look, or hoping fresh colours will fix quiet sales, it's a distraction dressed up as strategy. Boredom is the single most expensive reason to rebrand, because you feel the fatigue years before your customers ever would.

It can, if you change your domain or website structure carelessly. Google treats a new domain as a fresh start unless you set up proper redirects from every old page to its new home, and it takes time to recover the trust the old site had built. A visual rebrand that keeps the same domain and URLs is low-risk. A name change that moves you to a new web address needs a planned migration — redirects, updated listings, and patience — or you can quietly lose rankings you spent years earning.
