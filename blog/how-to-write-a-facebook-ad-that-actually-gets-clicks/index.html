How to write a Facebook ad that actually gets clicks

By the lemonlolly team·5 September 2026·5 min read

You boosted a post. It was a nice photo — the shopfront on Prospect Road, decent light, everyone smiling. Eighty dollars later you had reach in the thousands, a handful of likes, and not one enquiry. So you concluded that Facebook ads don't work for a business like yours.

Almost always, the ad wasn't broken. The writing was. Facebook ad copywriting is a specific little craft, and it has almost nothing to do with sounding professional. It's about earning about one and a half seconds of a stranger's attention while they're half-watching telly and scrolling with their thumb.

The job of the ad isn't to sell

This is the bit that trips up most business owners. You are not going to convince someone to spend $2,000 on a bathroom renovation inside a Facebook ad. That's not what the ad is for.

The ad has exactly one job: get the right person to take one small next step. A click, a message, a booking form. Everything else — the persuading, the proof, the pricing — happens after the click, on your website or in the inbox. Once you accept that, the writing gets a lot easier, because you stop trying to cram your entire sales pitch into a caption.

Write to earn the click, not to close the sale. The ad is the doorway, not the whole house.

The three parts every ad needs

Strip away the jargon and a working ad is three pieces stacked in order:

  • The hook — the first line, before the "…more" cut-off. This is what stops the thumb.
  • The offer — the specific thing you're giving them, in plain words.
  • The call to action — the one small step you want next.

That's it. No storytelling frameworks, no five-part formulas. If those three are solid, you have a functional ad. If any one of them is mush, the other two can't rescue it.

Writing a hook that survives the thumb

Facebook shows roughly one or two lines of your text before it collapses the rest behind a "…more" link that most people never tap. So your first line is effectively your whole ad.

The hooks that work tend to do one of three things: name the person you're talking to, name the problem they've already got, or say something specific enough to be surprising. The ones that don't work are the ones that talk about you.

Read your first line out loud. If it could sit on any competitor's ad with the name swapped out, it isn't a hook — it's wallpaper. The same first-line discipline applies on organic social too, which we went through in how to write captions that make people stop scrolling.

The offer is doing more work than the writing

Here's the uncomfortable truth about ad copy: a brilliant sentence attached to a boring offer will lose to a clumsy sentence attached to a good one, every time.

"Contact us for a quote" is not an offer. It's a request for the customer to do work. Compare it with "send us a photo of the room and we'll ballpark it by tomorrow" — same outcome for you, dramatically less effort for them, and far more likely to get a click.

Good offers tend to share three traits. They're specific about what the person gets. They're low-risk, so saying yes doesn't feel like a commitment. And they're easy — one photo, one form, one reply, not a phone call at a time you choose.

Ask for one small thing

Your call to action should name the action and, ideally, the next step after it. "Book now" is thin. "Tap through, pick a time that suits, and we'll confirm within the hour" tells someone exactly what happens if they click, which quietly removes the fear that they're about to be phone-stalked by a salesperson.

One CTA per ad. If you ask people to book, call, follow you and download the guide, they'll do the easiest of the four — which is scroll past.

The quiet click-killers

A few things reliably drag a decent ad down:

  1. Writing for everybody. An ad that speaks to all of Adelaide speaks to nobody. Narrow it — the suburb, the situation, the type of customer.
  2. Leading with your business name. Nobody scrolling has heard of you yet. Earn the attention first, then introduce yourself.
  3. Too many exclamation marks. They read as desperation, not enthusiasm.
  4. Mismatched landing page. If the ad promises Thursday appointments in Norwood and the link dumps them on your homepage, you've paid for a click and thrown it away.
  5. Stock photos. A slightly imperfect photo of your actual team beats a polished photo of strangers. People can smell a stock image instantly.

Write five, run three, keep one

Nobody writes the winning ad first go — not us, not anyone. The reliable method is volume then subtraction.

Write five different hooks for the same offer. Not five polished ads; five first lines, deliberately different from each other. One angle on price, one on speed, one on a problem, one on a specific customer, one that's a bit cheeky. Run three of them against the same audience with the same budget, leave them alone for a week or so, then keep whichever earns the cheapest click and bin the rest.

Resist the urge to fiddle daily. Small budgets need time to gather enough data to mean anything, and switching things off after two days tells you nothing except that you're impatient. We covered how far a modest budget actually stretches in Meta ads on a $500/month budget.

Being honest about what copy can and can't fix

Good writing lifts a decent offer. It cannot rescue a bad one, and it can't make Facebook the right channel if it isn't.

If people are already actively searching for what you sell — emergency plumbing, a locksmith, a specific product — you may be better off putting the money where the intent already exists. That trade-off is the whole argument in Facebook vs Google Ads: which should you start with. And the only benchmark worth measuring your click-through against is your own account last month, not a number you read in a blog post written about someone else's business in another country.

Write for one person, offer them one thing, ask for one small step. That's most of it.

Longer copy earns its keep when you're asking for something bigger and need to handle doubts first. What matters far more than length is that the first line does its job, because everything after it is hidden behind a “…more” that most people never tap.

The exception is when your pricing genuinely depends on the job — a builder, say — where a number without context does more harm than good. In that case give a range or a starting-from figure rather than nothing at all.

Nine times out of ten it's the offer, not the writing. Plenty of people are seeing the ad and deciding it isn't for them, which usually means you're asking for too much too soon, or you're saying something any competitor could also say. Before you rewrite a single sentence, ask whether a stranger would care about what you're offering. If the honest answer is no, better copy won't save it.
