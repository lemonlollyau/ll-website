How to grow on Instagram without buying followers

By the lemonlolly team·25 August 2026·5 min read

There's a moment most business owners hit around the four-hundred-follower mark. Growth has stalled, posting feels like shouting into a cupboard, and an ad appears offering five thousand followers for forty dollars. It is extremely tempting.

Don't. Not for moral reasons — for arithmetic ones. Bought followers are accounts that will never like, comment, save or buy. Instagram works out fairly quickly that a large share of the people it shows your posts to are ignoring them, and it draws the obvious conclusion: this content isn't interesting. You end up with a bigger number and less reach than you started with, which is the worst of both worlds.

A thousand followers who ignore you is worse than a hundred who don't. The number is decoration; the engagement is the engine.

What "growth" should actually mean for a local business

Before the tactics, a reframe. If you're a hairdresser in Glenelg, ten thousand followers is not the goal and never was. Six hundred locals who see your work and book a cut is a phenomenal outcome. Follower count is a proxy metric, and a bad one.

Better things to watch: saves, shares, profile visits, and the number of people who message you. Saves and shares in particular tell Instagram the post was worth keeping or passing on, which is what actually earns you more reach.

Five habits that grow a real audience

None of these are clever. All of them work, and they compound.

  • Post things worth saving or sending. A useful tip, a genuinely impressive result, a piece of local information. "Great week everyone!" gets neither.
  • Reply to every comment and message, quickly. Conversation is a signal, and it's also just good manners. Ten minutes a day here does more than an hour of scrolling.
  • Comment on other local accounts as your business. Not spam — actual, human comments on suppliers, neighbouring businesses, community pages. It's how new people find you, and it's free.
  • Use Stories for the ordinary stuff. Stories are where your existing audience stays warm. Feed posts are where new people meet you. Both matter, and they do genuinely different jobs.
  • Make Reels for reach. Video is still the format Instagram pushes hardest to non-followers. One a week beats none.

Fix the profile before you chase followers

People do visit your profile before they follow you, and most small business profiles quietly fail the test. Spend twenty minutes on:

ElementWhat it should do
Name fieldSay what you do and where — it's searchable, unlike your bio
BioOne line on who you help, one on what to do next
LinkStraight to booking or contact, not the homepage
HighlightsPrices, FAQs, before-and-afters, how to book
Top nine postsYour best work — this is your shopfront window

Putting "hairdresser Glenelg" in the name field rather than just your business name is one of the few genuine quick wins left on the platform. People search Instagram like a directory more than you'd think.

Hashtags, honestly

Hashtags matter far less than they did, and they were never the growth lever people claimed. Use five to ten that actually describe the post and the place — a mix of your trade, your suburb and your city. Skip the thirty-tag blocks and skip anything with millions of posts; you'll be buried in seconds.

They're a small nudge, not a strategy. If a post isn't good, no hashtag saves it.

Collaborate locally — the underrated one

The fastest honest growth for a local business is borrowing someone else's audience. A joint post with a neighbouring business, a supplier featuring your work, a shout-out swap with a complementary trade — a builder and an interior designer, a florist and a venue, a cafe and a roaster.

Instagram's collaboration feature puts one post on both accounts' profiles and in front of both audiences. It costs nothing, it's genuinely useful for both parties, and the followers you gain are local and relevant by definition. That's what makes it better than any paid shortcut.

Three things that quietly stall growth

If you're posting consistently and still flat, it's usually one of these rather than the algorithm having it in for you.

You're only talking to people who already know you. If every post is an update for existing customers, there's no reason for the platform to show it to anyone new. At least one post a week should make sense to a total stranger.

Your best work isn't on the grid. A lot of businesses bury their most impressive results in Stories, where they vanish in a day. If it's good, it goes in the feed.

You're inconsistent in bursts. Five posts in a week, then a fortnight of nothing, reads as abandoned to anyone who lands on your profile mid-gap.

Set a realistic pace and stop refreshing

Steady organic growth for a small local account looks like a handful of new followers a week, with occasional jumps when a post travels. It is slow, and it is meant to be slow. The compensation is that those people are real, nearby and capable of buying something.

Give it three months of consistent posting before you judge whether it's working — and pick a cadence you can hold rather than one that sounds impressive, which is the whole argument in how often you should really be posting. If you're stuck for what to actually make, an hour of planning will get you a month ahead.

The businesses that grow on Instagram aren't the ones with the best tactics. They're the ones still posting in month nine.

Instagram growth questions, answered

It does, in a practical rather than punitive way. Instagram decides how far to push a post partly on how the people who see it react. Fill your audience with accounts that never engage and the proportion reacting drops, so your reach drops with it. You've paid for a bigger number and less visibility, and cleaning it up afterwards is slow work.
