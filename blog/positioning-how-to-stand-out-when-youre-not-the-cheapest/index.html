Positioning: how to stand out when you're not the cheapest

By the lemonlolly team·5 August 2026·5 min read

You send a quote, the customer goes quiet, and a week later you find out they went with the mob down the road who came in two hundred bucks cheaper. It stings, and the tempting lesson is "next time, be cheaper." That lesson will slowly bleed you dry.

Here's the thing most local businesses never quite sort out: if the only reason someone can name for choosing you is price, then price is the only thing keeping them — and there's always someone hungrier, newer or more desperate willing to go lower. Positioning is how you give people a better reason. It's choosing a spot in the market that's yours, so you stop competing on the one number that only ever goes down.

What positioning actually means

Positioning is the answer to a simple question: why you, and not the ten others who do roughly the same thing? It's the corner of the market you decide to own in the customer's head — the fast one, the specialist, the one who actually turns up, the premium option, the local one who gets it.

It's a close cousin of brand. If you've read our piece on what a brand actually is, positioning is the strategic bit underneath — the decision about where you sit and who you're for, which everything else then hangs off. Get it right and your pricing, your marketing and your sales conversations all get easier, because you've stopped trying to be everything to everyone.

Why "cheapest" is a trap

Being the cheapest feels like a strategy. It's really just a queue you join at the back of. There's almost always someone who can undercut you — a bigger operator with better buying power, a newcomer buying market share, or a hobbyist who doesn't count their own time. Win on price and you win the customers who'll drop you for the next two-dollar saving.

Worse, low price eats the margin you need to do the work well. Skimp on materials, rush the job, skip the follow-up — and now you're cheap and average, which is nobody's favourite. The businesses that last don't win the price war. They opt out of it.

If price is the only reason a customer can give for choosing you, price is the only thing keeping them. Give them a better reason.

Find the position you can own

A strong position sits where three things overlap: something you're genuinely good at, something a specific group of customers actually wants, and something your competitors aren't already claiming loudly. Here are the angles most small businesses can plant a flag in:

  • The specialist. Not "we do everything" but "we're the ones for this." A sparkie who only does heritage homes. A bookkeeper who only works with tradies. Narrow feels scary; it's actually how you become the obvious choice.
  • The experience. Same service, better feeling. Easy booking, clear communication, no nasty surprises on the invoice. Plenty of trades win purely on "they actually called me back."
  • The speed. Same-day, next-day, done-by-Friday. If you can reliably be the fast one, that's a position people pay for.
  • The guarantee. Take the risk off the customer. A promise others won't match tells people you back yourself.
  • The local one. For an Adelaide business up against a national chain or an interstate franchise, "we're from here, we're across the corner of town you're in, and you can actually talk to the owner" is a real edge — if you mean it.

You don't need all five. You need one you can own and defend, then the discipline to keep pointing at it.

Write it down in one sentence

Once you've picked your angle, force it into a single plain sentence. A simple shape that works: For [who], we're the [category] that [what's different], so they [payoff].

For example: "For busy Adelaide cafés, we're the maintenance crew that fixes gear same-day, so they never lose a morning's trade." Notice there's no mention of price. It names who it's for, what's different, and why that matters — and it quietly tells the wrong-fit customers to look elsewhere, which is a feature, not a bug.

If you're stuck on the wording, it usually means the position itself is fuzzy, not the sentence. And once you've nailed it, it becomes the backbone of your brand voice and even the way you talk about your business name — everything gets easier to write once you know what you stand for.

Weak vs strong positioning

The difference usually isn't talent. It's specificity. Vague positioning tries to appeal to everyone and lands with no one.

Weak (forgettable)Strong (ownable)
"Quality work at competitive prices""Heritage-home electrical, done by a licensed specialist"
"Friendly local service you can trust""Same-day café equipment repairs across Adelaide"
"Affordable marketing for small business""Marketing for Adelaide trades who hate marketing"

Read the right-hand column and you instantly know who it's for and why. Read the left and you've already forgotten it. Neither mentions being cheap — the strong ones don't have to.

Make the position show up everywhere

A position you keep in your head does nothing. It has to be the first thing a customer meets — on your homepage headline, in your Google Business Profile description, in how you answer "so what do you do?" at a barbecue. Consistency is what turns a nice sentence into a reputation.

The test that it's working is lovely and simple: customers start describing you back to you the way you'd describe yourself. When people say "oh, you're the ones who always turn up on time" without you prompting them, the position has landed. If the only thing anyone remembers is your price, it hasn't — yet.

The honest catch

Positioning only works if it's true. You can't claim "the fast one" and then take three days to return a call, or "the specialist" while taking any job that walks in. Pick a position you can actually deliver day in, day out, because the market will fact-check you whether you like it or not. Choose something real, say it clearly, and repeat it until it sticks.

Positioning questions, answered

It's the easiest way to win price-shoppers, who leave the moment someone undercuts you. Cutting price also cuts the margin you need to do good work. Positioning wins customers who choose you for a reason other than being cheapest — and they tend to stay longer, spend more and complain less.

There almost always is, and that's fine. Positioning isn't about being the only one — it's about being the obvious choice for a particular kind of customer. Pick a niche, a style, a guarantee or an experience the others aren't claiming, and own that corner rather than fighting for the whole market.

Listen to how customers describe you and why they chose you. If they repeat the same reason back — your speed, your niche, your service — the position has landed. If they only ever mention price, it hasn't yet, and that's your signal to sharpen it.
