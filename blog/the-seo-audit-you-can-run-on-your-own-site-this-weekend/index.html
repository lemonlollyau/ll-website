The SEO audit you can run on your own site this weekend

By the lemonlolly team·30 July 2026·6 min read

It's Saturday morning. The shop's quiet, the coffee's still hot, and you've finally carved out an hour to work out why the website enquiries have gone a bit thin. The good news: you don't need a paid tool, an agency, or a computer science degree to find most of what's holding your site back on Google. You need a checklist, a phone, and about two hours.

This is the same DIY SEO audit checklist we quietly run on our own clients' sites before we ever suggest paying us for anything. It won't turn you into a technical wizard, and it won't catch every last thing a deep tool would. But it will surface the handful of problems that are actually costing you rankings and enquiries — which, for most Adelaide small businesses, are frustratingly ordinary and completely fixable.

Grab a notepad. Write down what you find as you go, because the point of an audit isn't to feel bad about your site — it's to walk away with a short, honest to-do list.

First, what an SEO audit actually checks

Strip away the jargon and an audit answers four plain questions. Can Google find and read your site? Does what Google shows in the results make anyone want to click? Does the page load quickly and work on a phone? And does your business look trustworthy and local to both Google and a human? Everything below hangs off one of those four questions.

You'll want three free things open: Google Search Console (if you haven't set it up, do that first — it's the single most useful window into how Google sees you), Google's PageSpeed Insights, and your own phone. That's the whole toolkit.

Check 1 — Can Google even find you?

There's no point optimising a page Google can't see. Start with the crudest possible test: go to Google and search site:yourdomain.com.au (swap in your real address). The number of results is roughly how many of your pages Google has in its index.

  • If your important pages — home, services, contact, key suburb or product pages — all show up, good.
  • If a page you care about is missing, it's either brand new, blocked, or Google has decided it's not worth indexing. Search Console's "Pages" report will tell you which.
  • If you see pages you didn't expect — old test pages, duplicates, thank-you pages — make a note. Clutter dilutes you.

Then open Search Console and look at the Pages report for anything marked "not indexed". A few reasons there are normal. A pile of your money pages sitting in that list is a red flag worth chasing.

Check 2 — Do your search results earn the click?

Google can rank you beautifully and you'll still get nothing if the title and description it shows look like wallpaper. Search for your business name, then for a service you offer, and read your own listing the way a stranger would.

Your page title and meta description are the shopfront window of your search result. If they don't make someone stop and click, your ranking is just an expensive participation trophy.

Go page by page and check that each has its own title that leads with what the page is actually about, ideally with a location, and a description that reads like a human wrote it. If two pages share the same title, or a title just says your business name and nothing else, you've found an easy win. We've written a full walkthrough on meta titles and descriptions if you want the exact formula.

Check 3 — Speed and the phone test

Most of your visitors are on a phone, often on patchy mobile data somewhere in the suburbs. Drop your homepage URL into Google's PageSpeed Insights and read the mobile score, not the desktop one. You're not chasing a perfect 100 — you're looking for anything genuinely broken.

Then do the test no tool can do for you: open your own site on your phone as if you were a customer. Time how long it takes to become usable. Try to tap the phone number. Try to find your address. Try to send an enquiry with your thumbs. If any of that is annoying, it's annoying for everyone, and Google notices behaviour like people bouncing straight back to the results.

If the score is grim, the usual culprits are huge unoptimised images and a pile of plugins or tracking scripts. That's worth its own afternoon — here's why slow pages cost Adelaide businesses customers and what to do about it.

Check 4 — Your local signals

For a local business, this is often where the real ranking movement hides. Three things to eyeball:

  • Google Business Profile. Is it claimed, complete, and in the right category? Are the hours current? Has anyone posted a photo this decade?
  • NAP consistency. That's your Name, Address and Phone number. Check they're written identically on your website, your Google profile, and any directory listings. Mismatches — an old suite number here, a different phone there — quietly confuse Google about who you are.
  • Reviews. Recent, responded-to reviews signal a living business. A profile with four reviews from 2021 does not.

Check 5 — Content and keywords

Last, read your main pages and ask a blunt question: would someone searching for what you sell actually find these words? A lot of small business sites are written in the language of the owner ("bespoke holistic solutions") rather than the language of the customer ("blocked drain Prospect"). Google matches the customer's words, not yours.

Look for pages that are painfully thin — a services page with two sentences — and pages that don't exist at all for services you offer. Both are gaps. If you're not sure what your customers actually type, our guide to keyword research for a local business walks through finding out in an afternoon.

What to do with what you found

Resist the urge to fix everything at once. Look at your notepad and pick the three items that are both easy and high-impact — usually a couple of title rewrites, a Google Business Profile tidy-up, and one page of proper content. Do those this weekend while it's fresh.

Then put a reminder in your calendar to run the whole list again in six months. Search rewards the businesses that keep showing up and keep tidying, not the ones that panic once a year. An audit you actually act on, twice a year, beats a fancy report that sits in your inbox.

That's the honest version: no dark arts, no guarantees, just a steady checklist a busy owner can work through between coffees. Most of what you'll find will be ordinary. Fixing it is where the enquiries come back.

DIY SEO audit questions, answered

No. Everything in this checklist can be done with free tools — Google Search Console, Google's PageSpeed Insights, and your own eyes on a phone. Paid tools are faster and dig deeper, but for a small local business the free ones catch the vast majority of what's actually costing you rankings and enquiries.

A full run-through once or twice a year is plenty for most small businesses, plus a five-minute weekly glance at Search Console. Search doesn't change fast enough to obsess over. The one time you should always audit is straight after a big change — a redesign, a move, or a rebrand — because that's when things quietly break.

Give yourself about two hours for the first one, because you'll be learning where everything lives. After that, a repeat audit takes under an hour. It's less about speed and more about working through the list honestly instead of skipping the bits that look hard.
