The complete social media strategy for local businesses

By the lemonlolly team·19 August 2026·9 min read

Ask a small business owner in Adelaide what their social media strategy is and you'll usually get one of two answers. Either a slightly embarrassed "we just post when we remember", or a list of platforms — "we're on Instagram, Facebook, and I set up a TikTok but I've never used it."

Neither of those is a strategy. The first is a habit and the second is an inventory. A strategy is a small set of decisions you make once, so that every Tuesday night you're not starting from a blank screen and a vague sense of guilt.

This is the whole thing, start to finish. It's six decisions. You can make them in an afternoon and they'll hold for a year.

First, decide what social is actually for

This is the step everyone skips, and skipping it is why most small business accounts drift into posting nothing in particular.

Social media can do several jobs, but not all of them at once, and not equally well. For a local business it's usually one of these:

  • Familiarity. When someone finally needs a plumber, a day spa, a builder — yours is the name that surfaces because they've seen you fifty times. This is the big one, and it's what social does better than any other channel.
  • Proof. Someone's been referred to you and they check your profile before they call. What they see decides whether they call.
  • Recruiting. Trades and hospitality especially. A visible, decent-looking business gets better applicants.
  • Direct sales. Real for product businesses with something to click through to. Mostly a fantasy for service businesses, and chasing it is how people end up disappointed.

Pick one primary job and one secondary. If your answer is familiarity and proof — which it is for most service businesses — then a lot of things stop mattering. Follower count stops mattering. Going viral stops mattering. Whether your last post got 40 likes or 400 stops mattering. What matters is that you turn up regularly and look like a business someone would trust with their money.

Most local businesses don't need to be famous on social. They need to be familiar to about four hundred people who live within twenty minutes of them.

Second, pick your platforms — properly

The correct number of platforms for a small business is one or two. Not four. Four is how you end up doing all of them badly and quietly resenting the whole exercise.

Choose on two criteria: where your customers actually are, and what kind of content you can realistically make. If you can't take photos, Instagram will be a grind. If you hate being on camera, TikTok is not for you, and that's a perfectly legitimate reason to skip it.

PlatformBest forWhat it costs you
InstagramAnything visual — food, beauty, retail, trades with good before-and-aftersYou need photos. Consistently.
FacebookLocal services, older customers, community groupsLow. Often the same content as Instagram, reworded.
LinkedInSelling to other businessesWriting, not photos. Slower but higher value per follower.
TikTokReaching people who've never heard of youHigh. Video, frequently, or it does nothing.
Google Business ProfilePeople searching for what you sell, right nowVery low. Criminally underused.

That last row isn't strictly social media, but it belongs in this conversation because it's usually the highest-return thing on the list for a local business. A post there reaches someone already looking to buy. If you only ever do one thing, do that one — our 12-point Google Business Profile checklist covers setting it up so it earns its keep.

Third, build three or four content pillars

A content pillar is just a recurring type of post. Having three or four means you never open the app wondering what on earth to say — you look at your list and pick the one you haven't done in a while.

A good pillar set for a local business usually includes:

  • The work. Finished jobs, before-and-afters, the product on the shelf. Proof you're good at the thing.
  • The people. Who works there, what a day looks like, the dog that lives in the workshop. This is what makes you a business rather than a logo.
  • The useful. Something a customer genuinely wants to know — how to care for the thing, what to check before you book, what a job like this usually costs.
  • The ask. Openings this week, a new product, a promotion. Roughly one post in five, no more.

The ratio matters more than the categories. If more than a fifth of your posts are asking for something, people learn to scroll past you. If none of them ever ask, you've built an audience that doesn't know you're available.

Write your pillars down somewhere you'll see them. They should sound like your business, not like a marketing template — if you haven't settled how you talk yet, finding your brand voice is worth doing before you write another caption, because it makes every future post faster to write.

Fourth, set a cadence you can hold on a bad week

Here's the rule we keep coming back to: the right frequency is the most you can sustain for six months without hating it. Not the most you can do in a good week.

For most small businesses that lands around two to three feed posts a week, Stories on a few days in between, and a Google Business Profile post each fortnight. If that sounds low compared to the advice you've read, it's because most of that advice was written by people whose entire job is posting. We went through how to find your own number — including a four-week test you can run on yourself — in how often a small business should really post.

Whatever number you land on, set it one notch below your ceiling. The headroom is the whole point. It's what stops the strategy collapsing the first time you have a genuinely awful fortnight.

Fifth, make it in batches or don't bother

Nobody who posts consistently is making posts one at a time. They sit down once a fortnight, make six, schedule them, and forget about it.

A batch session that works looks roughly like this:

  1. Gather. Ten minutes going through the photos on your phone from the last fortnight. This works if you've been taking them as you go, so make that the habit — a photo before, a photo after, on every job.
  2. Pick six. One from each pillar, then two more from whichever pillar has the best material this fortnight.
  3. Write short. Two or three sentences each. First line does the work; the rest is detail for the people who care.
  4. Schedule the lot. Spread across two weeks. Meta's own scheduler is free and does the job.
  5. Close the laptop. You're done for a fortnight.

If you're staring at your photo roll with nothing usable in it, the problem is upstream — you're not capturing as you work. Our list of 15 Instagram content ideas for local businesses is deliberately full of things you can shoot without staging anything or booking a photographer.

Sixth, measure four things and ignore the rest

Social platforms will show you dozens of numbers, almost all of which exist to make you feel something rather than to tell you something. Check these once a month, in about ten minutes:

What to checkWhat it tells you
Saves and sharesWhether the content was genuinely useful, not just pleasant
Profile visitsWhether people are curious enough to look you up
Link clicks or direction requestsWhether curiosity is turning into intent
Enquiries that mention socialThe only number that pays wages. Ask people how they found you.

That last one is the honest one, and it needs a human to collect it. Add "how did you hear about us?" to your booking form or just ask on the phone. Social media attribution is genuinely difficult — someone might see you eight times over four months and then Google your name — so the platform's own numbers will always undercount it, and any agency telling you they can trace it precisely is overselling.

The four ways this usually goes wrong

We've watched enough local accounts to know the failure modes are boringly consistent. If your social isn't working, it's probably one of these rather than anything to do with the algorithm.

Spreading too thin. Four platforms, none of them properly fed. The fix is subtraction, and it feels worse than it is — nobody has ever noticed a business quietly stopping on a platform they weren't using anyway.

Posting for other businesses. A surprising amount of small business content is unconsciously aimed at competitors and peers rather than customers. Industry in-jokes, awards, milestone announcements. Nice for you, invisible to the person deciding where to book.

Restarting instead of continuing. Three good weeks, a two-month gap, then a "we're back!" post and a fresh burst of enthusiasm. That cycle never compounds. It's better to drop to one post a week permanently than to keep sprinting and stalling.

Judging it by the wrong number. A post that reaches 200 locals and prompts two enquiries is a better post than one that reaches 5,000 people scattered across the country and prompts none. Reach that isn't local is mostly decorative for a business that only serves one city.

If you recognise more than two of those, don't overhaul everything at once. Fix the platform count first — it makes the other three easier.

Your first thirty days

If you're starting from a standstill, don't try to do all six at once. In order:

  • Week one: decide the job and the platforms. Delete or archive the accounts you're not going to use, so they stop making you feel bad.
  • Week two: write your pillars. Take twenty photos with no intention of posting them yet, just to build the habit.
  • Week three: run your first batch session. Schedule six posts.
  • Week four: set up your Google Business Profile posts and note your starting numbers so you've got something to compare against.

Then leave it alone for three months. This is the hardest instruction in the whole article. Social rewards patience in a way that feels almost unfair — nothing much appears to happen for a season, and then people start saying "I see you everywhere" when you've been posting twice a week the entire time.

The short version

Decide what social is for. Pick one or two platforms and properly abandon the rest. Write three or four content pillars so you never start from nothing. Set a cadence you could hold in your worst month. Batch it fortnightly. Check four numbers monthly and ask customers how they found you.

That's the strategy. It fits on a Post-it, which is roughly the length a strategy should be if anyone's actually going to follow it.

Social media strategy questions, answered

You need about one page of decisions, not a document. Which one or two platforms you'll use, what job social is doing for the business, three or four things you post about, how often, and which numbers you'll check each month. Write those down and you've got a strategy. Everything longer than that tends to be written to impress someone rather than to be followed.

The one your customers already use, which for most Adelaide service and retail businesses is Instagram or Facebook. If you sell to other businesses, LinkedIn usually beats both. The more useful question is which platform you can post to properly every week, because one account you actually maintain outperforms four you neglect.

Expect three to six months before it feels like it's doing anything, and closer to a year before it's clearly contributing. Social builds familiarity, and familiarity is slow. If you need enquiries this month, put your money into search — Google Business Profile and ads — and let social do the longer job underneath it.
