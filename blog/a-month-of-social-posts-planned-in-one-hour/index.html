A month of social posts, planned in one hour

By the lemonlolly team·23 August 2026·6 min read

It's Sunday night. You've had a good week, the phone's been busy, and somewhere between the washing and the emails you remember you haven't posted anything since Tuesday. So you open Instagram, scroll for inspiration, find none, and post a photo of the shop with a caption that says "Great week!" — and you feel a bit rubbish about it.

That's not a discipline problem. That's a planning problem. Deciding what to post is the hard bit, and doing it at 9pm on a Sunday is the worst possible moment to do it. The fix is to make all those decisions once, in one sitting, while you're fresh — and then spend the rest of the month just posting.

Here's the one-hour version. Set a timer, make a coffee, and by the end of it you'll have a month of posts mapped out.

Why an hour of planning beats an hour of posting

Most small business owners spend more than an hour a month on social media. They just spend it in five-minute panics, spread across thirty days, each one starting from a blank screen.

Batching the thinking works for the same reason batching anything works: switching costs are brutal. When you plan twelve posts at once you're in one headspace, you can see the shape of the month, and you notice the gaps — three photos of the same thing, nothing about the actual service you're trying to sell, nothing that answers the question every customer asks you.

You're not trying to fill a calendar. You're trying to stop deciding what to post while you're tired.

Minutes 0–10: pick four content pillars

A content pillar is just a recurring type of post. Four is the magic number — enough variety that you're not boring, few enough that you can hold them in your head.

Most local businesses can build a strong month from these four:

  • Proof. The work itself. Before-and-afters, finished jobs, a happy customer, a review screenshot.
  • Teach. Something you know that your customers don't. The question you answer every week on the phone.
  • People. You, your team, the dog that comes to work on Fridays. This is the one that builds actual attachment.
  • Offer. The thing you'd like people to buy. Booking availability, a seasonal service, a new product.

Write your four down. Yours might be different — a cafe might swap "Teach" for "What's on today" — but keep it to four, and make sure one of them is unapologetically about selling something. If you've never nailed down how your business sounds when it talks, the brand voice exercise is worth an afternoon before you start writing captions.

Minutes 10–35: fill the grid

Here's the template. Three posts a week, twelve for the month, rotating through your pillars so nothing gets neglected.

WeekPost 1Post 2Post 3
Week 1Proof — recent jobTeach — common questionPeople — behind the scenes
Week 2Offer — what's bookableProof — customer resultTeach — a mistake to avoid
Week 3People — meet the teamProof — before and afterTeach — quick how-to
Week 4Offer — next month's datesProof — review or testimonialPeople — something local

Copy that into a spreadsheet, a notes app, or a piece of paper stuck to the fridge. It genuinely doesn't matter which. Then go row by row and replace each generic label with an actual, specific post. Not "Proof — recent job" but "the Norwood bathroom, tiles before and after". Not "Teach — common question" but "how long does a colour actually take".

Specific is the whole trick. A vague plan is the same as no plan, because come Tuesday you'll still be standing there deciding.

Minutes 35–50: batch the assets

Now you know what you need, go get it. Walk around with your phone and shoot the twelve things — or as many as you can. Photos of the work, a short clip of you talking, the team, the shopfront in decent light.

Fifteen minutes with a list is worth more than an hour without one, because you're not wandering about hoping something photogenic happens. If you're shooting video, a phone is genuinely fine; the thing that separates good phone video from bad is light and sound, not the camera.

Anything you can't shoot today, note what's missing and when you'll get it. "Photo of the Prospect job — Thursday when it's finished" is a task. "Take more photos" is a wish.

Minutes 50–60: write and schedule

Write the captions while the photos are fresh. You don't need twelve masterpieces — you need twelve first lines that make someone stop, and the first line is where nearly all the work goes.

Then schedule what you can. Meta's own scheduler handles Instagram and Facebook for free. Whatever you use, get at least the first fortnight loaded, because the month always gets busier than you expect and the posts you've scheduled are the ones that survive.

If three posts a week feels like a lot, do two. A cadence you can hold beats a cadence you admire — we've argued that case at length in how often you should really be posting.

When the month goes sideways — and it will

Let's be honest about what a calendar can't do. It can't make a post go viral, it won't stop you getting slammed for a fortnight, and it doesn't cover the good stuff that happens unplanned — the customer who says something lovely, the job that turns out beautifully, the storm that closes the road.

Treat the plan as a floor, not a ceiling. If something real happens, post it and push the planned one back a day. The plan's job is to make sure that on the ordinary weeks, when nothing especially interesting occurs, you still show up. That's most weeks.

And if you miss a few? Nothing happens. No penalty, no algorithm grudge. You pick it up on the next one.

Do it again next month

The second month takes about forty minutes, because you already have your pillars and you know what kind of post lands. By the third you'll have a rhythm — plan on the first Monday, shoot on the first Wednesday, schedule the same afternoon.

That's the whole thing. Not more effort, just effort moved to a better moment. And if you want the bigger picture of how the posting fits with everything else you're doing, the complete social media strategy guide is the map this calendar sits inside.

Content calendar questions, answered

Twelve — roughly three a week — is a comfortable target for most local businesses, and it's what this template is built around. If that feels like too much, drop to eight and keep them good. Consistency at a lower number beats a burst of daily posting followed by three weeks of silence, both for your audience and for your own sanity.

It's a recurring type of post, so you're not inventing a new idea from scratch every time. Four works well for a small business: proof of your work, something useful you can teach, the people behind the business, and what you'd like people to buy. Every post you plan slots into one of them.
