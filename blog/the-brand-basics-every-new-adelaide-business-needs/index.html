The brand basics every new Adelaide business needs

By the lemonlolly team·6 August 2026·6 min read

You've registered the business name, sorted the ABN, and someone's cousin has knocked up a logo. Now what? A lot of new Adelaide business owners hit this exact point, feel like their "brand" is a bit thin, and either panic-spend on a full identity package they can't really judge, or freeze and do nothing at all.

Neither is the move. There's a small, boring set of brand foundations that every new business needs — a starter kit, really — and once they're written down and used everywhere, you'll look far more sorted than the number of years you've been trading. This is that kit, in plain English, in the order worth doing it.

First, the thing everyone gets wrong

Your brand is not your logo. The logo is the picture on the sign; the brand is the whole impression people carry around after they've dealt with you — what you do, who it's for, how you sound, and what they'd say about you to a mate. We've pulled that apart properly in what a brand actually is (it's not your logo), and it's worth a read if this is new territory.

Why does that matter for a starter kit? Because it stops you sinking your whole budget into a mark and neglecting the parts that actually make people remember and trust you. The logo is one line on the list below, not the whole list.

The starter kit: six foundations

Here's the lot. None of it needs a big budget, and most of it you can draft yourself over a weekend with a coffee and a notes app.

  • A clear name — one people can spell, say and find. If you're still deciding, our guide on naming a business so people actually remember it covers the traps.
  • A one-line description — what you do and who it's for, in a sentence a stranger would understand. "We're a mobile dog groomer for busy inner-west families" beats "premium pet care solutions" every day of the week.
  • Two or three colours — a main one, a contrast, and maybe an accent. That's it. Restraint reads as confidence.
  • One or two fonts — one for headings, one for body text. Pick them once and never think about it again.
  • A logo that works small — it has to be legible on a phone screen and a business card, not just a big banner.
  • A short note on how you sound — friendly? no-nonsense? a bit cheeky? Write down three words and a couple of "we say this, not that" examples.

That last one is the sleeper hit. Most new businesses nail the visuals and then write like a corporate press release, which undoes all the warmth. If you want a quick way in, finding your brand voice in one afternoon walks through it.

You don't need a brand that's finished. You need a brand that's consistent. A modest kit used the same way everywhere beats a beautiful one you're too unsure to apply.

How long each bit actually takes

People imagine branding is a months-long saga. For the basics, it isn't. Here's a realistic read on effort so you can knock it over rather than dread it.

Where new businesses trip up

The most common mistake isn't picking the "wrong" colour or an average logo. It's inconsistency — a Facebook page in one palette, a Google Business Profile with a different logo, an Instagram bio that sounds like a stranger wrote it, and a quote email in Times New Roman. Each touchpoint is fine on its own, but together they make you look like three different businesses.

The fix costs nothing. Put your six foundations in a single one-page document — colours with their hex codes, your fonts, the logo files, the one-liner, the three voice words — and use it as the reference every time you set up a new profile or send something out. That page is your brand starter kit. It's the difference between looking scattered and looking like you've got your act together.

Don't gold-plate it

It's tempting, once you get going, to keep polishing — a second logo variation, a mood board, a fifty-page guidelines document. Skip it for now. A brand new business doesn't need brand guidelines a bank would be proud of; it needs to be recognisable and to keep showing up. You can always upgrade later, and the honest truth is you'll make better calls about a bigger investment once you've traded for a year and learned who your customers actually are.

Save the fancy stuff for when you've earned the problem of having outgrown the basics. That's a good problem, and a long way off for most.

Start with the one-pager

If you do nothing else this week, open a blank document and fill in the six foundations. Even rough answers beat none — you can refine as you go. The goal isn't a perfect brand; it's a written, repeatable one that every part of your business points back to.

Get that down and you're ahead of a surprising number of established Adelaide businesses who've been winging it for years. Then it's just a matter of using it, everywhere, every time.

New-business branding questions, answered

Six things, and none of them is an expensive rebrand: a clear name, a one-line description of what you do and for whom, two or three brand colours, one or two fonts, a consistent logo, and a short note on how you sound. Get those written down and used everywhere, and you'll look more sorted than most businesses twice your age.

No. Early on, consistency beats polish every time. A simple logo used the same way everywhere, a tidy set of colours and a clear description will do more for you than a pricey identity you're too nervous to change. Spend the money later, once you know who you are and what's working — a full rebrand is worth it when you've outgrown the basics, not before.

Your logo is a small part of your brand — the picture on the sign. Your brand is the whole impression people carry around: what you do, who it's for, how you sound, whether you turn up looking the same each time, and what folks say about you when you're not in the room. The logo helps them recognise you; the brand is the reason they remember and trust you.
