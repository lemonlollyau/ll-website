How often should a small business really post on social media?

By the lemonlolly team·17 August 2026·5 min read

It's ten past nine on a Tuesday night. You've closed the till, answered the last three enquiries, and now you're staring at your phone thinking I haven't posted in a fortnight. So you throw up a photo of something, anything, caption it "Great day today!", and feel vaguely bad about the whole business of social media until the next time guilt strikes.

Almost every small business owner we meet in Adelaide is running that exact loop. And the advice they've been given — post daily, post twice daily, feed the algorithm — is what put them there. It's advice built for people whose whole job is posting.

The honest answer

The right frequency is the most you can keep up for six months without hating it.

That's it. Not the number in a guru's carousel. Not what your competitor down the road is doing. The number you can still hit in November when you're flat out, in January when you're away, and in the middle of a week where three jobs go sideways at once.

Because the thing that actually works on social isn't volume — it's showing up often enough, for long enough, that people stop thinking of you as a stranger. Posting nine times in one week and then vanishing for two months does nothing for that. One decent post a week for a year does an enormous amount.

Three posts a week that you'll still be making at Christmas beats seven a week that you'll abandon by the end of the month. Boring, but true.

What "enough" looks like, platform by platform

If you want a starting point rather than a philosophy, here's what we suggest to most small local businesses. Treat it as a floor to build from, not a target to feel guilty about.

PlatformSensible cadenceWhy
Instagram feed2–3 a weekEnough to stay familiar; Stories fill the gaps
Instagram Stories2–4 days a weekLow effort, high familiarity, disappears in 24 hours
Facebook page2–3 a weekOften the same content, reshaped for a slightly older crowd
LinkedIn1–2 a weekOnly worth it if you sell to other businesses
TikTok3+ a week, or don't botherThe one platform where low frequency genuinely underperforms
Google Business Profile posts1 a fortnightQuietly the highest-value one for local search

Two things worth saying plainly. First, you don't need to be on all of these — pick the one or two where your customers actually are and do those properly. Second, if you can only manage one thing on this list, make it the last one. A Google Business Profile post reaches people who are already searching for what you sell, which is a much shorter walk to a booking than a story view. There's more on that in our 12-point Google Business Profile checklist.

Find your real number in four weeks

Rather than guessing, run a short test on yourself. It takes a month and it settles the question for good.

  1. Week one: post three times. Note how long each one took you, start to finish, including the faffing about with the caption.
  2. Week two: same three, but batch them — sit down once and make all three in one go.
  3. Week three: try four, batched.
  4. Week four: drop back to two and notice whether it feels like relief or like slacking off.

At the end you'll know two things you didn't before: roughly how many minutes a post costs you, and where your genuine ceiling sits. Set your cadence one notch below that ceiling. The headroom is what stops you quitting in a busy month.

Batching is the whole trick

Nobody who posts consistently is making posts one at a time. They're doing it in a single sitting — an hour with a coffee, once a fortnight — and scheduling the lot.

A workable batch session looks like this: pull the photos you've already taken on jobs or in the shop, pick six, write six short captions, schedule them across the next two weeks, done. The photos are the bottleneck, so build the habit of taking them as you go. If you're stuck for what to shoot, our 15 Instagram content ideas for local businesses is a list of things you can photograph without staging anything.

Scheduling in advance also fixes the other problem with guilt-posting: the quality of a 9pm Tuesday post is never as good as the quality of a post you made calmly on a Sunday with three others.

What to do when you miss

You will miss. A week will disappear and nothing will go out. Here's the important bit: don't try to make it up.

Posting four times on Monday to catch up doesn't restore anything — it just annoys the people who follow you and burns the content you'd have used next week. Skip it, post your normal one or two, carry on. Consistency isn't a streak you break; it's an average you hold over months. We wrote about that idea in a broader sense in the boring secret behind memorable brands, and it applies here more than anywhere.

Signs you should change your number

Once you've settled at a cadence, leave it alone for a few months. After that, there are only really two signals worth acting on.

  • Post more when the posts you're making are consistently getting saved, shared or replied to, and you've got a comfortable backlog of photos sitting in your phone. That's a sign there's demand for more of you.
  • Post less when you've started padding — reposting quotes, sharing other people's memes, posting something because it's Thursday rather than because it's worth seeing. Filler doesn't build anything, and it makes the good posts harder to spot.

And be honest about what you're measuring. Follower count is the least useful number on the page. Saves, shares, profile visits and messages tell you whether anyone's actually moving towards buying something. If a post with 40 likes brings in one enquiry, it beat the post with 400 likes that brought none.

The short version

Pick a number you can hold on your worst week, not your best. Batch it so it costs you one sitting a fortnight instead of six panics. Skip the catch-up posts. Watch saves and messages rather than likes. And if all of that still feels like too much, do one thing a week and one Google Business Profile post a fortnight — that's a perfectly respectable social media presence for a small business, and it's about forty minutes a month.

The businesses that win on social locally aren't the loudest ones. They're the ones still there in a year.

Posting frequency questions, answered

Two to three times a week is a sensible target for most small businesses, with Stories on the days in between if you have them. That's frequent enough to stay familiar and slow enough that you can keep it up when the business gets busy. If two feels like a stretch right now, start at one a week and hold it for a month before you add more.

It's not fatal, but it's the point where social stops doing much for you. Below weekly, most people who follow you simply won't see you often enough to remember you exist, and you lose the main benefit — being the business that springs to mind when someone finally needs what you sell. If weekly is genuinely impossible, put that time into your Google Business Profile and your website instead, where the work keeps paying out without you.

You can reuse the idea, but reshape it for each place. The same job photo can be a carousel on Instagram, a short written story on Facebook, and a lesson-style post on LinkedIn. What looks lazy is a copied caption with hashtags stacked at the bottom on a platform where nobody uses them. Same substance, different clothes.
