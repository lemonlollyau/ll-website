How to handle a negative comment or review publicly

By the lemonlolly team·4 September 2026·5 min read

It always seems to land on a Sunday. You're halfway through a coffee somewhere on Prospect Road, you check your phone out of habit, and there it is. One star. Three sentences. Something about the wait, or the price, or a staff member having an off day. Your stomach drops and your thumbs start moving before your brain catches up.

Put the phone down. Not forever — you should reply, and reasonably quickly — but not in the first ten minutes. What you write next matters more than the review itself, and it's worth getting right.

The reply isn't for the person who wrote it

This is the reframe that makes the whole thing easier. You are not writing to the reviewer. You're writing to the next forty people who read that review while they're deciding whether to book you.

Most of those people worked out long ago that one grumpy review among sixty good ones is normal — a business with nothing but five stars looks stage-managed. What they're actually doing is scrolling down to see how you handled it. A calm, specific, human reply does more for you than the bad review does against you. A defensive one does the opposite, and it does it in public, permanently.

A bad review costs you a little. A bad reply to a bad review costs you a lot. That's the whole game.

Wait — but don't wait too long

Aim to reply within a day. Long enough that you're not typing while your ears are hot; soon enough that it doesn't look like you're hiding.

If it's a rough one, write the reply, save it somewhere, and read it again after dinner. Better still, have someone who wasn't involved read it first. Nine times out of ten they'll delete a sentence, and it'll be the sentence you were most attached to.

The four-part reply that works nearly every time

Almost every good public response does the same four things, in the same order:

  1. Thank them, and name the specific thing. Not "thanks for your feedback" — that's wallpaper. "Thanks for telling us about the forty-minute wait on Saturday." It proves a person read it.
  2. Own what's actually yours. If you got it wrong, say so plainly. If you didn't, you can still be sorry about their experience without confessing to something that didn't happen.
  3. Say what's changed. One concrete thing. "We've added a second person to the Saturday morning shift." This is the sentence future readers care about most.
  4. Offer to take it further, off the platform. A name and an email address. Then stop replying in public.

Put together, it's short. Three or four sentences, not an essay:

Thanks for flagging the wait on Saturday, Ben — forty minutes is well over what we aim for and I'm sorry you copped it. We were a person short and we've since added a second on the Saturday morning shift. If you'd like to give us another go, email me at hello@ and I'll sort you out. — Sam, owner

Sign it with a real name. It changes the temperature of the whole thing.

The words that make it worse

Some phrases read fine in your head and terribly on a screen. Avoid:

  • "As I explained to you at the time…" — you've just started arguing in front of customers.
  • "We have no record of your visit." — even when it's true, it reads as calling them a liar.
  • "This is clearly a fake review." — say it to the platform, not the public.
  • Identical copy-pasted replies under every review. People scroll. It's obvious, and it undoes the point of replying.
  • "Unfortunately, our policy is…" — policy language makes a small problem sound institutional.
  • Anything describing the customer's behaviour. Their tone is not the subject. Yours is.

When it's unfair, or fake, or a competitor

It happens. Report it through your Google Business Profile, then reply anyway — briefly, neutrally, no accusations. Something like: "We can't find a booking under this name, but we'd genuinely like to look into it. Email hello@ and we'll sort it."

Be realistic about removals. Google takes down reviews that break its policies — abuse, spam, off-topic rants, conflicts of interest — not reviews that are merely unflattering or unfair. Reports can take weeks, and often go nowhere. Meanwhile your reply is doing the work, so make it a good one. And don't organise a mass-reporting campaign among your mates; it doesn't help and it can rebound on your profile.

Social comments play by different rules

A comment under a Facebook post isn't a review, and treating it like one will make you look stiff.

The temptation to delete is enormous and almost always wrong. Delete a genuine complaint and you get a screenshot, a repost, and a second round that's worse than the first. Hide is for spam, abuse, and anything that names a staff member — that's a duty-of-care call, not a reputation one.

Make it a non-event next time

The businesses that handle this well aren't calmer by nature. They've just built three boring things in advance.

First, a steady trickle of new reviews, so one bad one is a blip rather than a quarter of your total — that's the whole point of asking for reviews without being annoying. Second, a written note on how you sound, so whoever's holding the phone replies in the same voice you'd use; an afternoon spent pinning down your brand voice covers it. Third, one named person who owns the inbox and the comments, with a rough time each day to check them — the sort of thing that lives in your social media strategy rather than in someone's head.

Do those three and the Sunday-morning one-star stops being a crisis. It becomes a small job, handled by lunchtime, that quietly makes you look better than the review made you look bad.

Negative review questions, answered

Reply to every one that describes a real experience, even if it's only two lines. Silence reads as 'we didn't care enough to answer', and future customers notice that gap more than you'd think. The exception is abuse, or something clearly not about your business — report those and leave them alone.

Only if it breaks Google's policies — fake, abusive, off-topic, or posted by someone with a conflict of interest. Google won't remove a review just because it's unfair or you disagree with it. Report it through your Business Profile, expect it to take a while, and reply publicly in the meantime so readers aren't left with only one side of the story.

Hide rather than delete, and only for spam, abuse, or anything that names a staff member. Deleting a genuine complaint tends to escalate it — people screenshot, repost, and the second round is worse than the first. Answer once in public, then offer to sort it out in a message.
