How to find your brand voice in one afternoon

By the lemonlolly team·3 August 2026·5 min read

You sit down to write your About page. Or a caption. Or the fortnightly email you keep meaning to send. You type a sentence, read it back, and it sounds like it could belong to any business in Adelaide — a bit stiff, a bit safe, a bit like a brochure from 2009. So you delete it and try again, and the second go sounds exactly the same.

That's not a writing problem. It's a voice problem. You haven't decided how your business sounds yet, so every time you open a blank box you default to Generic Business Voice — the beige one everybody uses. The good news is you can fix it in an afternoon, and you don't need a copywriter, a workshop, or a fancy brand strategist to do it.

A quick note before we start: voice is one slice of a bigger thing. If you haven't yet, it's worth reading what a brand actually is — because your voice only works when it's pointing in the same direction as everything else you do.

What brand voice actually is (and what it isn't)

Your brand voice is the consistent personality that shows up in your words — everywhere from your homepage to the reply you leave on a three-star review. It's not what you say. It's how you sound saying it.

People muddle voice up with tone, so here's the clean split. Voice is your personality: it stays the same no matter what. Tone is your mood: it shifts with the moment. You're the same person at a funeral and at a barbecue — that's your voice. How you actually speak at each — quieter here, louder there — that's your tone.

Your voice is the part of you that stays the same whether you're apologising for a stuffed-up order or announcing your best sale of the year.

Why bother? Because a recognisable voice is one of the cheapest ways a small business builds trust. When you sound like a real, specific human — the same one, every time — people feel like they already know you. When you sound like a template, they feel nothing, and feeling nothing is a very short trip to scrolling past.

Step one: pin down three words

Grab a scrap of paper and write three adjectives that describe how you want to come across. That's the whole exercise. The catch is that most people pick the same limp three — "professional, friendly, reliable" — which describe roughly every business that has ever existed and therefore describe none.

Make them sharper with the "but not" trick. Add a boundary to each word so it actually means something:

  • Warm, but not soft — we're kind, not a pushover.
  • Confident, but not corporate — we know our stuff without the buzzwords.
  • Playful, but not childish — a bit of cheek, never silly for its own sake.

Those are ours, roughly. Yours will be different, and they should be. Three words with edges will steer more of your writing than a whole page of brand waffle ever could.

Step two: raid what you already say

Here's the bit that saves you the afternoon: your voice already exists. It's living in how you talk to customers when you're not trying to "write". You just have to go and find it.

Open your phone's voice recorder and explain what you do to an imaginary mate — out loud, no script. Then look at the places you already sound like yourself:

  • Voice memos or notes where you explain your service off the cuff.
  • The way you answer the same question at the counter for the hundredth time.
  • Your best replies to Google reviews — the honest, human ones.
  • How you text a regular customer you actually like.

Read those back and you'll spot the phrases that are unmistakably you: the way you explain things, the jokes you reach for, the words you'd never be caught using. That raw material is your voice. The job isn't to invent one — it's to notice the one you've got and do it on purpose.

Step three: draw the line with a "sounds like / never sounds like" table

A voice is defined as much by what it rejects as what it embraces. Sit down and write a handful of before-and-afters — the beige version next to the you version. It makes the whole thing concrete fast.

We sound likeWe never sound like
"Reckon your website could be working harder? Let's have a look.""Leverage our synergistic digital solutions."
"Here's the honest answer, and here's what we'd fix first.""Please find attached our comprehensive proposal."
"That one's not worth your money — save it for something that'll move the needle.""We offer a full suite of premium service tiers."

Five or six rows and you'll feel the shape of it. This table does more heavy lifting than any adjective list, because it shows the voice in the wild instead of describing it in the abstract.

Step four: write it down so it survives Monday

An afternoon's clarity is worthless if it's gone by next week. Put it on one page — genuinely one page, not a forty-slide guideline nobody opens:

  • Your three words, each with its "but not".
  • A short do / don't list (short sentences, no jargon; contractions are fine; never use the word "solutions").
  • Two or three example sentences straight from your table.

That one-pager is the whole system. Stick it above your desk or pin it in your notes app, and read it for ten seconds before you write anything that matters.

Step five: test it on something real this afternoon

Don't let this stay theoretical. Pick one live piece of writing and rewrite it in your new voice while it's fresh. Two good candidates: the first two lines of your homepage — see how to write a homepage that Google and humans both love for how voice and clarity share the space up top — or a single caption you'd normally agonise over.

Read the rewrite out loud. If it sounds like something you'd actually say to a customer standing in front of you, you've got it. If it sounds like a press release, you've slipped back into beige — go again. The same instinct applies to the bigger stuff too, like naming your business: the words that stick are the ones that sound like a person, not a committee.

Keeping your voice alive when someone else writes for you

The moment you hand a caption to a VA, a designer, or a freelancer, your voice is at risk — not because they're careless, but because they can't hear the version in your head. The fix is the one-pager. Hand it over with every brief and it does the remembering for you, so a stand-in sounds like you on a busy week instead of reverting to the beige default.

That's the whole point of writing it down: a voice that only lives in your head can't scale past you. A voice on a page can.

Brand voice questions, answered

Your brand voice is the consistent personality that comes through in everything you write — your website, captions, emails and replies. It's not what you say, it's how you sound saying it. A clear voice makes a small business recognisable and trustworthy, even before anyone reads the details.

Voice stays the same; tone shifts with the situation. Your voice is your personality — warm, direct, a bit cheeky, whatever fits you. Tone is the mood you bring to a specific moment, like a gentle apology for a mistake versus an excited launch announcement. Same voice, different tone.

Yes — for most small businesses the voice already exists in how you talk to customers day to day. An afternoon is enough to pin down three defining words, pull real phrases from how you actually speak, and write a one-page guide. Refining it happens over time, but you can have a usable voice by dinner.
