Should your business be on TikTok? An Adelaide reality check

By the lemonlolly team·24 August 2026·5 min read

Someone at a barbecue tells you their cousin's cafe blew up on TikTok. A rep rings and says you're missing out. Your niece says nobody uses Facebook any more. And you go to bed wondering whether you should be dancing on the internet at forty-three.

Short answer: probably not, and definitely not the dancing. Longer answer: TikTok is a real channel that genuinely works for some Adelaide businesses and is a complete waste of a Tuesday for others. The trick is working out honestly which one you are before you sink three months into it.

What TikTok is actually good at

Forget the dances. What TikTok does better than anything else is put a video in front of people who have never heard of you. Instagram mostly shows your stuff to people who already follow you; TikTok's feed is built around interest, not connection, so a video from a business with two hundred followers can land in front of thousands of strangers.

That's the appeal, and it's a real one. It's also the catch: reach isn't the same as customers. A thousand strangers watching your video is worth nothing if nine hundred of them live in Manchester.

Who it's genuinely worth it for

TikTok tends to pay off for businesses where one or more of these is true:

  • The work is visual and satisfying to watch. Cleaning, renovating, detailing, baking, hairdressing, landscaping. If people would stop and watch you do it in real life, they'll stop and watch it on a phone.
  • Your customers are broadly under forty. Not a hard rule, and the platform's age range keeps widening, but if your buyers are retirees you're fishing in the wrong pond.
  • You can sell to anyone, anywhere. Products you can post, online services, anything not tied to a postcode. National reach is a feature, not a rounding error.
  • Someone in the business actually enjoys it. This matters more than people admit. TikTok rewards volume and personality, and neither survives being a chore.

Who can safely skip it

And here's the other side, which nobody selling social media services will tell you.

If you're...The honest read
A local trade booked out for six weeksSkip it. Your bottleneck is capacity, not awareness.
Selling to other businessesMostly skip it. LinkedIn and referrals will do more.
A professional service — legal, financial, medicalSkip or tread carefully. Compliance and tone are a real problem.
Already stretched keeping one platform goingSkip it. Fix the one you're on first.
Reliant on customers within 15kmOnly if you can be relentlessly local in the content.

That last one is the Adelaide-specific catch. National reach sounds lovely until you realise a plumber in Findon needs about four hundred locals, not forty thousand strangers. You can absolutely work locally on TikTok — name the suburbs, film recognisable places, talk about things only people here would get — but you have to do it deliberately.

Reach you can't sell to is a hobby, not a marketing channel. Lovely, but don't put it in the business plan.

The cheap way to test it

You don't have to decide in the abstract. Run a small, time-boxed test and let the results argue.

  1. Give it six weeks and two videos a week. Twelve videos is enough to see a pattern. Less than that and you're reading noise.
  2. Reuse what you're already making. If you're shooting for Reels, you already have the footage — the formats overlap almost entirely, and the Reels-versus-Stories-versus-posts breakdown covers which idea suits which slot.
  3. Make it obviously local in at least half of them. Say the suburb. Show the street. Let the algorithm and the viewer both work out where you are.
  4. Measure enquiries, not views. Add "found us on TikTok" to how you ask new customers where they came from. Views are the vanity number; the phone ringing is the real one.

At the end of six weeks you'll have a clear answer, and it cost you maybe three hours total. That's a far better way to settle the question than another year of wondering.

If you do go ahead, three things that matter

Hook in the first second. Not the first five — the first one. Show the mess before the clean, the problem before the fix. The same thinking applies as writing a caption first line, just faster.

Sound matters more than picture. Phone cameras are excellent now; phone microphones are not, especially outdoors or in a tiled room. If people can't hear you, they leave.

Post more than feels comfortable. TikTok is a volume game in a way Instagram isn't. Two a week is a test. If the test works, you'll need more than that to build anything.

The honest conclusion

TikTok is not a magic tap and it is not beneath you. It's a channel with a particular shape: brilliant for visual, entertaining, geographically flexible businesses run by someone who doesn't mind being on camera. Mediocre for everyone else, and actively a distraction if your existing channels are half-finished.

If you're not sure where it sits in the wider picture, start with the complete social media strategy for local businesses and work out which platforms you're choosing on purpose. Being deliberately absent from a platform is a perfectly respectable strategy. Being vaguely absent while feeling guilty about it is not.
