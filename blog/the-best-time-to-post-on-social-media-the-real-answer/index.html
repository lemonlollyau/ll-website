The best time to post on social media (the real answer)

By the lemonlolly team·29 August 2026·5 min read

Search "best time to post on social media" and you'll get a hundred confident charts. Tuesday 11am. Wednesday 9am. Thursday between 1 and 3. They all disagree with each other, and almost all of them were built from American accounts posting in American time zones.

Adelaide, with its gloriously awkward half-hour offset, is not well served by any of it. So here's the honest version: there is no universal best time, there is a best time for your audience, and you can find it in about ten minutes with tools you already have.

Why the charts don't work for you

Those studies average millions of posts across every industry, country and account size. What comes out the other end is a number that describes nobody in particular.

A bakery in Norwood and a commercial electrician in Wingfield have almost nothing in common in this respect. One's audience is scrolling at 7am deciding where to get coffee; the other's is scrolling at 8pm on the couch after the kids are down. Averaging those two together gives you a time that's wrong for both.

There's also a self-defeating problem with popular advice: if everyone posts at the "best" time, that's the most crowded slot in the feed.

What actually decides who sees your post

Modern feeds aren't chronological, so posting time doesn't work the way people assume. It matters indirectly, through a chain like this:

  • You post. The platform shows it to a small slice of your audience first.
  • If that slice engages quickly — likes, saves, comments, watch time — the platform shows it to more people.
  • If it lands flat, distribution quietly stops.

So the goal isn't to hit a magic hour. It's to post when enough of your people are awake and holding their phones, so that first slice has someone to land on. That's a much lower bar, and a much more forgiving one.

Timing doesn't make a mediocre post travel. It just stops a good post from being born into an empty room.

Find your own best time in ten minutes

Every platform will tell you when your followers are online. You just have to go and look.

PlatformWhere to lookWhat you'll get
InstagramProfessional dashboard → Total followers → Most active timesActive hours by day of week
FacebookMeta Business Suite → Insights → AudienceSimilar hourly breakdown
LinkedInPage analytics → UpdatesEngagement by post, not by hour — infer it
Any of themYour own last 20 posts, sorted by reachThe most honest data you have

That last row is the one people skip and it's the best one. Your own top-performing posts already know something. Write down the day and hour of your ten best and your ten worst, and see whether a pattern falls out.

Test it properly, or don't bother

One post at 7am proves nothing. A post's performance is driven far more by whether the content was any good than by the clock.

If you want a real answer, pick two candidate windows — say 7–8am and 6–7pm — and alternate between them for a month, keeping the type of content roughly consistent. Then compare the two groups, not individual posts. You want at least eight to ten posts in each bucket before you believe anything.

And check your own calendar while you're at it. If you're a cafe, your customers are looking at lunch options at 11:30, not at 3pm. Common sense beats analytics more often than anyone in marketing likes to admit.

The Adelaide wrinkle

Two things make imported advice worse than useless here.

The first is the half-hour offset. A lot of scheduling tools and dashboards default to somewhere else, and a "best time" chart built on eastern-states data is already thirty minutes out before it's wrong for any of the other reasons. Check your scheduler's timezone setting before you trust a single number in it.

The second is that Adelaide's rhythm genuinely differs. Traffic is lighter, so the commute window is shorter and earlier than Sydney's. School pick-up lands hard in the afternoon feed. And if your customers are tradies, they're on their phones at 6am and 3pm, not 9am and 5pm. None of that shows up in an international average, and all of it shows up in your own insights.

Different formats, different clocks

One more thing the charts flatten: not every format wants the same hour.

  • Stories are the most time-sensitive thing you post. They're gone in a day and they're consumed in bursts, so they want your audience's actual peak.
  • Reels and short video are the least time-sensitive. They keep being served for days or weeks, so posting time barely matters — make it good and let it travel.
  • Feed posts sit in the middle: distribution front-loads in the first few hours, then tapers.

Which is a decent argument for not overthinking the schedule of the thing that keeps working, and putting the effort into the thing that expires.

When timing genuinely does matter

There are cases where the hour really counts, and they're all time-sensitive rather than algorithmic:

  • Same-day offers. A "we've got six loaves left" Story is worthless at 4pm.
  • Events. Post while people can still decide to come.
  • Weather-driven trades. Storm damage, heatwave aircon, that sort of thing.
  • Anything with a deadline — bookings closing, last day of a sale.

Outside those, the difference between 9am and 11am is smaller than the difference between a good caption and a lazy one.

The thing that beats timing every time

If you only have limited energy for social, spend it in this order: post consistently, make the first line worth stopping for, then worry about the clock.

Consistency is what trains the platform (and your audience) to expect you — and there's an honest, achievable cadence in how often a small business should really post. Having posts ready in advance is what makes consistency survive a busy week, which is the whole point of planning a month in one sitting. And the single biggest lever on that early-engagement window is the first line of your caption, which is worth more attention than any chart — that's covered here.

Post when your people are awake. Post often enough to matter. Then stop reading timing articles, including this one, and go make something worth seeing.
