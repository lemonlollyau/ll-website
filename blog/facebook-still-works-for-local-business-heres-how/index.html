Facebook still works for local business — here's how

By the lemonlolly team·26 August 2026·5 min read

Ask a room of business owners about Facebook and someone will say it's dead. Then, ten minutes later, someone else will mention they got three jobs last month from a comment in a suburb community group. Both of those things are true at once, and the gap between them is where most local businesses are leaving money.

Facebook as a place to post pretty photos and hope is, fair enough, a shadow of what it was. Organic reach on a business page is thin and has been for years. But Facebook as a piece of local infrastructure — the place where your suburb asks each other for recommendations — is very much alive, and it skews older and more local than anywhere else you could be.

The bit that actually works: community groups

Every Adelaide suburb has at least one. Some have four, plus a "buy nothing", plus a noticeboard, plus one run by a bloke with strong opinions about parking. These groups are where people ask "can anyone recommend a decent electrician" and get eleven answers within the hour.

That recommendation thread is the single highest-intent moment in local marketing. Someone has a problem, they've asked publicly, and they're going to hire whoever gets vouched for. You cannot buy your way into that thread. You can, however, be the sort of business that keeps getting named in it.

How to do that without being the person everyone mutes:

  • Join as yourself, not as the business page. People trust a person. Put your trade in how you introduce yourself and leave it there.
  • Answer questions you're not going to get paid for. Someone asks what's causing a damp patch; tell them, honestly, including when it's not worth calling anyone. This is the whole game.
  • Read the group rules on promotion. Most allow you to respond when tagged or when someone asks for recommendations. Most ban unprompted ads, and enforce it.
  • Never argue in the comments. Ever. It's a small town and the screenshots travel.
Be the person who helps for free in the group, and you'll be the name people type when someone else asks. That's the entire mechanism.

Events, which almost nobody uses properly

Facebook Events still get decent distribution, and they show up in local event discovery. If you do anything that has a date attached — a workshop, a sale, a market stall, an open day, a launch, a talk — make it an Event rather than a post.

The difference is that an Event has a mechanism: people can mark interested, they get reminded, and their friends see they're going. A post has none of that. It appears, it's ignored, it's gone.

Even if the "event" is modest — extended trading hours, a one-day special, a guest in the shop — the format does work a plain post won't.

What to actually post on the page

Your page still matters, just not as a broadcast channel. It matters because people check it. They've heard your name, they look you up, and what they find decides whether they call.

What people checkWhat it should say
Is this business still going?Something posted in the last fortnight
Are they any good?Reviews and recent finished work
Are they open?Correct hours, including public holidays
How do I reach them?Phone number that works, messages answered

Two posts a fortnight is genuinely enough to pass that test. Cross-post from Instagram if that's your main channel — nobody's checking whether the photo appeared elsewhere first.

One thing worth doing properly: turn on messaging and actually answer it. A slow reply on Facebook Messenger is a lost job, and the response time is displayed publicly on your page.

Who Facebook suits best

It works hardest for businesses serving households in a defined area — trades, cleaners, mobile services, allied health, tutors, anything where "who do you use for X" is a question people ask their neighbours. It also over-indexes with customers over forty, which is a large slice of the people in Adelaide with money to spend on their homes.

It's weaker if you sell to businesses, if your customers are mostly under twenty-five, or if your product is visual enough that Instagram simply shows it better. Nothing wrong with choosing not to bother — the argument in the TikTok reality check applies just as much here, in reverse.

A modest weekly routine

Fifteen minutes, twice a week. Check your groups and answer anything you can genuinely help with. Reply to any comments or messages on the page. Post once, or cross-post from wherever you're already active.

That's it. It's not exciting and it won't produce a graph you can show anyone, but it quietly keeps you in the recommendation thread, and the recommendation thread is where local work comes from. Pair it with a steady trickle of Google reviews and you've covered the two places people check before they call.

Facebook isn't the shiny channel any more. For a lot of Adelaide businesses it's still the one that pays.

Facebook questions, answered

Your personal profile, in almost every case. People respond to a person, and most community group rules are written to keep business pages from broadcasting. Mention your trade when you introduce yourself and let your helpfulness do the rest — the recommendations follow the human, not the logo.

For most small pages, yes — only a small slice of your followers will see any given post, and that's been the case for years. Which is why the page's job has changed: it's less a broadcast channel and more a shopfront people check before they call you. Keep it current and answered rather than trying to win reach back.

Two posts a fortnight is enough for most local businesses. The bar you're clearing is 'this business is clearly still operating and looks decent', not 'this business dominates my feed'. Cross-posting from Instagram is completely fine and saves you inventing anything new.
