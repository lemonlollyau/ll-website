Backlinks for small businesses: the honest, doable version

By the lemonlolly team·28 July 2026·5 min read

You mention to someone that you're trying to climb Google, and within about a minute the word "backlinks" turns up. Then, often, a quote: an agency will happily build you links for a couple of thousand dollars a month, indefinitely, with a straight face. For a café in Norwood or a plumber in Salisbury, that's a genuinely mad thing to sign up for.

Here's the honest version. Backlinks matter, but not the way the scary sales pitch makes out — and the ones actually worth having are things you can earn yourself, for little or no money, from businesses and organisations you already know. No PR agency required. Let's clear the fog.

What a backlink actually is

A backlink is just another website linking to yours. That's it. If the Norwood Business Association lists your shop on their members page with a link, that's a backlink. If a supplier writes "we work with [your business]" and links to you, that's a backlink too.

Google treats each one a bit like a recommendation. When a site it already trusts links to you, some of that trust rubs off — the digital version of a respected local vouching for you. A link from the City of Adelaide website carries real weight. A link from "TopSEODirectory-Ranker-Pro dot biz" carries roughly none, and might even count against you.

One link from a website Google already respects is worth more than fifty from places it's never heard of. Quality beats quantity so heavily it isn't a fair fight.

Why the usual backlink advice is useless for you

Most backlink advice online is written for e-commerce brands and national companies chasing thousands of links across the country. It talks about "link-building campaigns", "outreach at scale" and "guest posting strategies" — all of which cost either a lot of money or a lot of hours you don't have.

A local service business doesn't need any of that. You're not trying to outrank the whole internet. You're trying to look trustworthy to Google in your patch of Adelaide, against a handful of local competitors. That's a much smaller, much more winnable game — and it rewards a few good local links far more than a mountain of generic ones.

The links you can actually get (without a PR budget)

Here's where the doable part comes in. Almost every small business is sitting on a pile of easy, legitimate links it simply hasn't claimed. Work through this lot:

  • Industry associations and memberships. If you belong to a trade body, chamber of commerce or professional register, most have a member directory that will link to your site. Ask them to add you, or check your existing listing has your web address.
  • Local directories that real people use. Not the spammy ones — the genuine ones. Your council's local business listings, established Adelaide "what's on" and community sites, and reputable national directories all count. These also help the consistency Google looks for when it decides who to show on Maps.
  • Suppliers and partners. The brands you stock, the software you're certified in, the trades you refer work to — many keep a "stockists" or "find a partner" page. A quick email asking to be added is often all it takes.
  • Local sponsorships. Sponsor a junior footy team, a school fete or a community event, and you'll usually land a thank-you link on their site. You were probably going to support them anyway — this just makes it count twice.
  • Genuine local press. Did something newsworthy — a milestone, a hire, a charity effort, a new location? A short note to a local paper or community newsletter can earn a mention with a link, and those tend to be strong ones.

Where to spend your effort first

Not every link is worth the same chase. Here's a rough guide to what pays off for the time it takes.

Association / membership directoryLowYes — quick and trusted
Supplier "stockists" pageLowYes — one email
Reputable local directoryLowYes — do a few, then stop
Local sponsorshipMediumYes — if it fits your budget anyway
Local press mentionMediumYes — strong links, worth the pitch
Paid link packagesAnyNo — risky and against the rules

Start at the top. The first three cost you an afternoon and almost nothing else, and they're the links Google trusts most for a local business anyway.

How to actually ask for a link

The asking bit is where most people freeze, so keep it simple and human. No template, no jargon, no "I was wondering if you'd be open to a collaboration opportunity." Just a normal note:

"Hi — we're a customer/member/partner of yours and we've just tidied up our website. If you keep a stockists or members page, would you mind adding us? Here's the link: [your address]. Thanks!"

That's it. Most people say yes, because you're making their page more useful and it costs them nothing. The trick is simply asking — the vast majority of these links never get built because nobody thinks to send the email.

The links to walk away from

A quick word of caution, because the internet is full of people who'll sell you links that do more harm than good. Steer clear of:

  • Anyone selling "100 backlinks for $50". These come from junk sites and can drag your rankings down, not up. It's the SEO equivalent of a dodgy discount extension cord — cheap, and a fire risk.
  • Link exchanges at scale. A natural link swap with one genuine local partner is fine. Signing up to networks that trade links in bulk is a pattern Google spots and dislikes.
  • Cold emails promising to "get you to page one" with links. Straight to the bin. If it were that easy and that cheap, everyone would already be on page one.

Where backlinks sit in the bigger picture

Here's the reassuring part: for most Adelaide businesses, backlinks aren't even the first thing to fix. Your Google Business Profile, your reviews and your on-page basics all move your local ranking faster and with less effort. Links are the steady, long-game layer you build up gently underneath.

So do the easy, honest links a few at a time — the membership listing, the supplier page, the sponsorship you were doing anyway — and then get back to running your business.
