How to shoot good social video on just your phone

By the lemonlolly team·27 August 2026·5 min read

You know you should be making video. You've read that it gets more reach, you've seen competitors doing it, and every time you try, the result looks like a hostage tape. Dark, echoey, slightly green, filmed from an unflattering angle in a room that turns out to be much messier on camera than in real life.

The good news is that the phone in your pocket has an excellent camera and is not the problem. The problem is almost always light, sound or framing — and all three are free to fix. Here's the whole lot, in the order it matters.

Light: the one that does the most

Ninety per cent of "my video looks cheap" is a lighting problem. Phone cameras are brilliant with plenty of light and grainy without it, and no editing fixes footage shot in the dark.

The rules are short:

  • Face the light. If there's a window, look at it. Never stand with it behind you or you'll be a silhouette.
  • Get outside, or near a door. Daylight is the best free light you'll ever have. An open roller door on a workshop is a beautiful softbox.
  • Avoid overhead lights on faces. Downlights make eye sockets look like caves. Turn them off and use the window.
  • Mind the middle of the day. Harsh Adelaide summer sun at noon is unflattering on everyone. Shade, or early and late, is kinder.

If you take one thing from this article: turn around so the light is on your face. That single move improves more videos than any piece of equipment.

Sound: the one people notice most

Here's the uncomfortable truth — viewers will forgive slightly ordinary footage and will not forgive bad audio. If they can't hear you cleanly, they scroll. Phone microphones are the weak link, and they're worst exactly where small businesses film: tiled rooms, workshops, footpaths, anywhere windy.

Free fixes, in order of impact:

  1. Get closer. Halve the distance to the phone and the difference is dramatic. Arm's length is far better than across the room.
  2. Kill the echo. Hard, empty rooms bounce sound. Anything soft — a rug, a curtain, a room with furniture in it — sounds better than a bathroom.
  3. Turn things off. Extractor fans, air conditioners, the radio. You stop hearing them; the microphone doesn't.
  4. Get out of the wind. Wind noise is unfixable in post. Stand behind something, or film indoors.
People will watch a slightly grainy video with clear audio. Nobody watches a beautiful video they can't hear.

If you end up making video regularly, a cheap clip-on microphone is the one purchase genuinely worth making. Not first, though — get the free things right and see if you keep it up.

Framing: hold it properly and stop wobbling

A few simple calls, and then stop thinking about it.

DecisionDo this
OrientationVertical for social. Horizontal only if it's going on your website.
Eye linePhone at eye height, not resting on your chest looking up your nose.
DistanceHead and shoulders for talking; wide enough to see the work for demos.
SteadinessLean on something, or prop the phone. Elbows in beats hand-held drift.
BackgroundLook at it before you press record. Actually look.

Use the rear camera rather than the selfie camera when you can — it's the better lens on every phone. Prop it up, hit record, and step back into frame.

Structure: the first second decides everything

Technically perfect video that opens with "Hi guys, so today I thought I'd talk about..." will lose to a wobbly one that opens with the finished bathroom. Front-load the interesting bit. Show the mess before the clean, the tangle before the fix, the result before the explanation.

It's the same discipline as writing a caption first line, which we pulled apart in how to write captions that make people stop scrolling — you're earning the next second, then the one after that.

Keep them short. Fifteen to thirty seconds is plenty for most business videos, and short means you'll actually make the next one.

Five things worth filming this week

If the blank-screen problem is what stops you, here are five that work for almost any local business and need no script:

  • A before and after — the most reliably watchable thing you own.
  • A close-up of the work happening. Hands doing the thing, no talking required.
  • The question you answer every week, straight to camera, thirty seconds.
  • A quick tour of the shop, the van, the workshop or the setup.
  • One thing people get wrong about your trade, and what to do instead.

None of those need a script, a set or a good hair day. They need light, quiet and about ninety seconds.

Add captions, always

Most people watch with the sound off, at least to begin with. Instagram and TikTok both generate captions automatically now, and they're decent — but check them, because they will absolutely mangle a trade term or a suburb name and it looks careless.

Batch it and stop overthinking

The businesses that end up with good video aren't the ones with the best setup. They're the ones who film six things in one go while they're already on site, in decent light, with a list of what they need — which is exactly what planning a month of posts in an hour gets you.

Your first videos will be a bit awkward. Everyone's are. By the tenth you'll have stopped noticing, and so will everyone watching.

Phone video questions, answered

Not to start. Filming near a window with the light on your face and standing closer to the phone will fix most problems for free. If you're still making video regularly after a couple of months, a cheap clip-on microphone is the single most useful purchase — audio is where phones are genuinely weak, whereas the camera is already good.

Vertically for anything going on Instagram, TikTok or Facebook, because that's how people hold their phones and the full screen is yours. Film horizontally only when the video is destined for your website or a presentation. If you're not sure, vertical is the safer bet for a small business.

Fifteen to thirty seconds covers most of what a local business needs to show. Longer is fine when you're genuinely teaching something, but short videos get finished, and a finished video is a much stronger signal than a long one people abandon. Short also means you're more likely to make the next one.
