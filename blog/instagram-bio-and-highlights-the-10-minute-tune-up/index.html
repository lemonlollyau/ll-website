Instagram bio and highlights: the 10-minute tune-up

By the lemonlolly team·31 August 2026·5 min read

Someone sees your post, likes the look of it, and taps your profile. What happens in the next three seconds decides whether they follow you, message you, or leave.

Most small business profiles lose them. Not because the business isn't good, but because the bio says something like "Adelaide based 😊 DM for enquiries" and the highlights are four circles labelled with grey default covers from 2023.

This is a ten-minute job with a better return than almost anything else you'll do on Instagram this month.

Your profile is a landing page

Treat the bio the way you'd treat the top of your website: it has to answer three questions fast — what is this, is it for me, and what do I do next. That's exactly the job the top of a website has to do, in a fraction of the space.

You get roughly 150 characters, one link, a name field, and a row of highlight circles. That's the whole shopfront.

Notice what's not in there: your mission statement, a list of emojis, the word "passionate", or "📧 DM for prices". If someone has to message you to find out whether they can afford you, most of them won't.

The name field is a search field

This is the bit almost everyone misses. Instagram's search matches against your username and your name field — not your bio text. So if your name field just says "Hutt St Dental", you'll only ever be found by people who already know your name.

Add what you do and where: "Hutt St Dental · Adelaide Dentist". Now you can turn up when someone searches "adelaide dentist" inside the app. It takes fifteen seconds and it's the single highest-value change on this page.

Keep it readable, though. "Adelaide Dentist Teeth Whitening Veneers Invisalign CBD" is keyword stuffing, it looks desperate, and it's the same instinct that doesn't work on Google either.

Your username is your name. Your name field is your job. Most businesses waste it saying their name twice.

The two bits above the bio

Before anyone reads a word, they've already judged two things.

The profile photo. It's displayed at about the size of a five-cent piece. A wide shot of your shopfront, a full logo with a tagline, or a team photo all turn to mush at that size. Use either a tight crop of your logo mark or a clear headshot if you're the face of the business. Squint at it on your phone; if you can't tell what it is in a glance, neither can anyone else.

The username. Short, spellable, and ideally the same handle you use everywhere else. If yours is @huttstdental_adelaide_official because the good one was taken years ago, it's worth checking whether the good one has since been abandoned. Changing a handle is mildly annoying and permanently worth it.

Highlights: the silent salesperson

Highlights are the only part of your profile that stays put. Used properly they answer the questions people are too shy to ask.

A good set for a local service business, in this order:

  • Start here — who you are, in four Stories.
  • Prices or How it works — the thing they actually want.
  • Results or Before & after — proof.
  • Reviews — screenshots of real ones.
  • FAQs — parking, timing, what to bring.

Three rules. Order matters, because only about five are visible without scrolling — put the money ones first. Covers should be plain and on-brand: a flat colour with a word on it beats a stock icon every time. And anything older than a year either gets refreshed or deleted, because a highlight full of 2023 opening hours reads as a business that's stopped paying attention.

The link

One link, pointing at the one thing you most want someone to do. Booking page, enquiry form, menu, price list.

Link-in-bio tools are fine if you genuinely have several equally important destinations. But most small businesses use one to offer five options because they can't decide, and every extra tap loses people. If you'd struggle to name your single most valuable next step, that's a positioning problem rather than a link problem.

The bio mistakes we see most

  • "DM for prices." A barrier dressed up as a conversation. Most people won't, and the ones who do are often just price-checking. Give a starting price or a range.
  • Saying your name twice. Username, name field and line one all reading "Hutt St Dental" wastes your only two searchable fields.
  • Emoji as punctuation. One or two as signposts is fine. A bio that's forty per cent emoji is harder to read and reads as less established, which is the opposite of what most businesses want.
  • Adjectives instead of facts. "Passionate about quality" tells a stranger nothing. "Fifteen years, fixed quotes, eastern suburbs" tells them everything.
  • An untouched link. Pointing at a homepage you last updated in 2023 undoes all of the above.

The ten-minute run-through

  1. Put your service and suburb in the name field.
  2. Rewrite line 1 so a stranger knows what you do.
  3. Add one line about who it's for, in your own words.
  4. Add one concrete offer or price if you have one.
  5. Point the link at a single action.
  6. Reorder highlights so the useful ones are first.
  7. Make five matching covers.
  8. Delete anything out of date.

Then send it to someone who doesn't know your business and ask them what you do and what they'd tap next. If they hesitate, you've still got work to do — and it's worth doing before you spend another month chasing new followers, because a leaky profile wastes every one of them.

Instagram profile questions, answered

One link, unless you genuinely have several equally important destinations. Most small businesses reach for a link-in-bio tool because they can't decide what matters most, and every extra tap loses people. Pick the single action worth the most to your business and send everyone there.

Barely. Bio text isn't matched in Instagram's general search — your username and name field are. Putting your service and suburb in the name field will do far more for discovery than a row of hashtags, which mostly just makes the bio harder to read.

Check them every few months, and delete anything more than a year old that isn't evergreen. Prices, hours and offers go stale fast, and an out-of-date highlight quietly tells visitors the business isn't paying attention. Evergreen ones like 'How it works' can sit for years if the content still holds up.
