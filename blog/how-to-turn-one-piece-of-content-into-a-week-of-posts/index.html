How to turn one piece of content into a week of posts

By the lemonlolly team·30 August 2026·9 min read

Here's the trap almost every small business falls into with social media. You sit down on Sunday night, open Instagram, and try to think of something to say. Nothing comes. You post a photo of the shopfront with a caption about the weather, feel a bit embarrassed, and close the app.

The problem isn't that you've run out of ideas. It's that you're trying to generate a brand new one every single day, which is a workload no small business can sustain and no marketing team would ever attempt.

Professionals don't do it that way. They make one substantial thing, then cut it up. Here's how to do the same.

The mistake: treating every post as a new idea

If you post five times a week across two platforms, inventing fresh each time means forty original ideas a month. That's a full-time content job.

Now flip it. Make one genuinely useful thing a week — a video, a customer story, a proper answer to a question you get asked constantly — and turn it into seven or eight posts. That's four anchors a month. Suddenly it's a Tuesday-morning job, not a second career.

There's a second benefit that people miss. Repetition is how messages land. Nobody sees everything you post; most followers see maybe one in ten. Saying the same important thing five different ways isn't lazy, it's how you actually get heard.

Your audience isn't sick of your message. Most of them haven't heard it once.

Pick the right anchor

Not everything splits well. A good anchor piece has three qualities:

  • It answers a real question. Something customers genuinely ask you, in the words they'd use.
  • It has parts. Steps, reasons, a before and an after, a list of mistakes. Parts are where the cuts happen.
  • It's yours. Filmed in your workshop, from your job, with your opinions. Generic advice repurposes into seven generic posts.

Good anchors for a local business: a job walk-through, a "three things people always get wrong about X", a customer's before-and-after, a short explainer on how your pricing works, a seasonal warning ("what to check before the first hot week").

Bad anchors: a quote graphic, a public holiday post, anything that could belong to any business in your industry.

The seven-way split

Once you've got the anchor, the cutting is mechanical. Here's the pattern, using a five-minute video as the source:

OutputHow you make itWhere it goes
The full pieceThe anchor itself, lightly editedFacebook, YouTube, your blog
Short clip 1The most surprising 30 secondsReels, TikTok
Short clip 2The practical how-to bitReels, TikTok
CarouselThe steps, one per slideInstagram, LinkedIn
Text postThe single strongest point, written outLinkedIn, Facebook
Photo + captionA still from the shoot, with the key tipInstagram feed
StoriesBehind the scenes of making it, plus a pollInstagram, Facebook

That's seven posts, and none of them required a new idea. The choice of format per platform isn't arbitrary either — each one does a different job, which is worth understanding properly if you want the cuts to land. Reels, Stories and feed posts each have their own use.

A worked example

Say you're a plumber. Your anchor is a five-minute phone video shot on a job: "Why your hot water system dies in July".

Out of that one video you get:

  1. The full video on Facebook, captioned with the three warning signs.
  2. A 25-second Reel: just the moment you point at the corroded fitting and say what it means.
  3. A second Reel: "three noises that mean call someone", counted off on your fingers.
  4. A carousel: five slides, one warning sign each, last slide a soft "if you've got two of these, get it looked at".
  5. A LinkedIn text post: the honest version — why most emergency call-outs in winter were avoidable in autumn.
  6. A feed photo: the old unit and the new one side by side, with what the repair actually cost.
  7. Stories: filming it, a poll ("do you know how old your hot water system is?"), and the answer.

One morning's filming. A week and a bit of posting. And every piece is genuinely useful, which is the part that makes it work.

Where to find anchors when your head is empty

The whole system depends on having something worth cutting up, and "think of a good idea" is not a reliable weekly input. So keep a list instead. Four places it fills itself from:

  • Your inbox and your phone. Every question a customer asks you twice is an anchor. Write it down in their words, not yours.
  • Your quotes. The objection that comes up in half your quotes — price, timing, whether it's worth doing at all — deserves a proper answer once, publicly.
  • Your jobs. Anything that went unusually well, unusually badly, or unusually strangely. Photograph it while it's happening; you'll never recreate it later.
  • The season. Adelaide gives you a free calendar: first hot week, first cold snap, Show week, Christmas shutdown, back-to-school. Each of those has a warning or a tip attached to it in your trade.

Ten minutes with a notes app and last month's emails will usually produce a quarter's worth of anchors. The bottleneck was never ideas; it was that nobody wrote them down when they appeared.

The bit of kit that speeds up the cutting

You don't need an editing suite. What actually saves time is deciding the cuts before you film. If you know you want two short clips and a carousel, you'll naturally deliver the surprising bit and the how-to bit as separate, self-contained chunks rather than one meandering five minutes that has to be salvaged in the edit.

Practically: say each key point twice, once slowly, and pause for a beat before and after. Those pauses are where the cuts go. It feels stilted while you're doing it and looks completely normal on screen.

How to repurpose without sounding repetitive

The risk is obvious: post the same thing seven times and people tune out. Four rules keep it fresh.

Change the angle, not just the format. Don't publish the same sentence as a video, a graphic and a caption. One piece leads with the problem, one with the fix, one with the cost, one with the mistake.

Space them out. Don't dump all seven in three days. Spread them over a week to ten days, and let the anchor breathe first.

Rewrite for the room. The same point sounds different on LinkedIn than it does on Instagram. Same substance, different register — which is much easier when you've already decided how your business sounds.

Let the good ones come back. If a piece performed, run it again in three months with a new opening line. Almost nobody will notice, and the ones who do won't mind.

The weekly rhythm

Here's the version that actually survives a busy week:

  • Tuesday, 45 minutes. Film or write the anchor. Phone is fine — good phone video is mostly light and sound, not gear.
  • Tuesday, 45 minutes. Cut the clips, write the captions, load everything into a scheduler.
  • Rest of the week. Reply to comments. That's it.

Ninety minutes, once a week. If that still sounds like a lot, it's roughly the time most owners already lose to staring at a blank caption box across the same seven days.

How to tell whether it's working

Repurposing changes your workload immediately and your results slowly, so judge it on the right things.

In the first month, the only honest measure is whether you actually posted. If you went from three posts a fortnight to five a week without dreading it, the system is doing its job.

From month two, look at which anchors performed, not which individual posts did. If the hot water system video produced two strong clips and a carousel that all did well, that's a topic worth returning to from a different angle in six weeks. If an anchor produced seven flat posts, the subject was the problem, not the format — and you've learned that cheaply.

By month three you should notice something more useful than metrics: people arriving at enquiries already half-convinced, quoting things back to you that you said in a video. That's the actual return, and no dashboard reports it.

What not to repurpose

A few things are better left as one-offs. Anything time-stamped — a public holiday, an event, a specific promotion — ages badly in a second life. Anything about a specific customer, unless they've cheerfully agreed to be a case study more than once. And anything you didn't really believe when you made it, which is the honest reason a lot of content never gets reused: nobody wants to hear it twice, including the person who made it.

Prices are the other trap. A carousel built around "from $180" will still be sitting in a highlight or a saved post long after that number has changed, and the first you'll hear of it is someone quoting it back to you on the phone. If a number is going to move, either leave it out of the evergreen cuts or diarise a reminder to pull them.

Where this fits

Repurposing is the engine, not the strategy. It only works if you've already decided who you're talking to, which platforms are worth your time and what you're actually known for — that's the job of a proper social media strategy. And it pairs naturally with batching, so the anchor for each week is chosen in advance rather than on the day; a month planned in one hour is the companion habit.

Do both and social stops being the thing you dread on a Sunday night. One good idea a week, cut seven ways, is enough. It always was.

Repurposing questions, answered

Only if you republish it identically. Done properly you change the angle each time — one post leads with the problem, one with the fix, one with the cost. And remember most followers see roughly one post in ten, so the repetition you're worried about is invisible to nearly everyone.

Seven or eight is a comfortable target from a five-minute video or a solid written piece: the full thing, two short clips, a carousel, a text post, a photo, and a run of Stories. Push much past ten and you start stretching thin material, which people notice.

Different formats and different wording, yes — the same substance, no. A point that works as a 25-second Reel also works as a LinkedIn paragraph, but the register is different. Rewriting the caption for each platform takes minutes; inventing separate ideas for each takes a job you don't have time for.
